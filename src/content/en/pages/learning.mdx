<h2 id="intro">About this section</h2>

Children learn from the people around them long before they start school, and much of that learning happens in everyday moments. This section brings together what research says about helping that along, from birth to about age 12: conversation, books, play, praise, motivation, focus and self-control, numbers, sleep and screens, and, for school-age children, homework and study habits.

Choose your child’s age above. Each age page starts with a few things to do now and ends with a one-page printable. You can also find all of them under <L to="/printables/#learning">Printables</L>.

Everything here comes from academic sources: meta-analyses, trials, long-term studies and the published policy statements of medical bodies, all listed on the <L to="/research/#group-learning">Research</L> page. Findings that rest on a single study, on studies that disagree, or on studies of older children are marked <Mixed /> so you can weigh them yourself. <L to="/research/#how-to-read">How to read the evidence</L> explains the difference between a link and a cause.

None of this needs special toys, apps or classes, and no family does all of it. Pick one or two ideas that fit your days.

<h2 id="principles">Big ideas for every age</h2>

<h3 id="conversation">Talk with your child, not just to them</h3>

Young children learn language through back-and-forth: they make a sound, look or point, you respond, and they take another turn. A review of infant research reports that parents’ responsiveness predicts babies’ word learning,<Cite id="tamis-lemonda-2014" /> and in a randomized trial, coaching mothers to respond more helped their babies’ social, emotional, communication and thinking skills grow.<Cite id="landry-2006" />

One long-term study that recorded families at home linked back-and-forth turns at 18-24 months with children’s language and IQ scores 10 years later, while the sheer number of words adults spoke was much less strongly linked once socioeconomic status was taken into account.<Cite id="gilkerson-2018" /> <Mixed />

<h3 id="books">Books, with your child talking too</h3>

A meta-analysis of studies on how often parents read to preschoolers linked shared reading with language growth, early literacy and reading achievement, whatever the family’s socioeconomic status.<Cite id="bus-1995" /> A meta-analysis of 19 trials that coached parents to share picture books found small gains in children’s language and a large gain in parents’ book-sharing skills, and programs that gave less coaching did little.<Cite id="dowdall-2020" />

Asking questions and letting the child do more of the telling is called dialogic reading. In a meta-analysis of 16 studies, it gave a moderate boost to the words children can say, compared with ordinary shared reading.<Cite id="mol-2008-dialogic" />

<h3 id="guided-play">Play, with a light guiding hand</h3>

The American Academy of Pediatrics describes play with parents and other children as a singular opportunity to build social, emotional, thinking, language and self-control skills.<Cite id="yogman-2018-play" /> In a meta-analysis, guided play (the child leads, an adult gently steers toward something to learn) did better than direct teaching for early math, shapes and switching between tasks, and better than free play for words about space and position, but no differences were found on other key outcomes.<Cite id="skene-2022-guided-play" /> <Mixed />

<h3 id="praise-process">Praise what your child did</h3>

In a study that observed 53 families at home, more praise for toddlers’ effort was linked with children believing, at 7-8 years old, that ability can grow, and with enjoying challenges more.<Cite id="gunderson-2013" /> <Mixed />

In experiments with 5- and 6-year-olds and with fifth graders, children praised for being clever, or for who they are rather than what they did, coped worse with a later setback;<Cite id="kamins-dweck-1999" /><Cite id="mueller-dweck-1998" /> yet the home study found that parents’ praise of traits was not linked with fixed beliefs later on.<Cite id="gunderson-2013" /> <Mixed />

So there is no need to police every word. The habit worth building is simple: say what your child did, such as “You kept trying different pieces until it fit.”

<h3 id="autonomy">Support your child’s own drive</h3>

Autonomy support means taking your child’s point of view seriously, offering real choices within your limits and explaining your reasons. A review stresses that it is not the same as permissiveness,<Cite id="joussemet-2008" /> and a meta-analysis of 36 studies linked parents’ autonomy support with higher achievement, more self-driven motivation and better psychological health.<Cite id="vasquez-2016" /> Across 41 studies, simply having a choice raised motivation, effort and performance, and the boost to motivation was bigger for children than for adults.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" />

Rewards are more debated. One meta-analysis found that rewards for doing, finishing or doing well at a task undermined later interest in it, and that tangible rewards tended to be more harmful for children than for college students;<Cite id="deci-1999" /> another concluded that rewards are generally not harmful.<Cite id="cameron-2001" /> <Mixed /> Both report that a tangible prize promised in advance for something already interesting can reduce interest, and that positive feedback can help, though one found it tended to help children less than college students.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" />

<h3 id="self-control">Focus and self-control can be practiced</h3>

Executive functions are the mental skills behind focus and self-control. A review names three core ones, holding back an impulse, holding information in mind (working memory) and switching flexibly between ideas, and notes that stress, lack of sleep, loneliness and lack of exercise all impair them.<Cite id="diamond-2013" />

A series of meta-analyses of programs for children up to age 12 found that these skills can be strengthened, but no convincing evidence that the gains last. Approaches that built the skills into other activities worked as well as or better than direct training, and the authors note they are more enjoyable for children.<Cite id="takacs-kassai-2019" /> <Mixed />

<h3 id="everyday-math">Numbers belong in everyday talk</h3>

A meta-analysis of 64 studies found a positive, statistically significant link between the home math environment (the math-related activities, talk, attitudes and expectations at home) and children’s math achievement, with an average correlation of r = .13 and large differences between studies.<Cite id="daucourt-2021" /> <L to="/research/#effect-sizes">Effect sizes</L> explains what numbers like this mean.

Counting things your child can see, especially groups of 4 to 10, was the kind of number talk linked with children’s later grasp of what number words mean.<Cite id="gunderson-levine-2011" /> <Mixed />

<h3 id="sleep-and-screens">Enough sleep, and screens in their place</h3>

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine, in a statement the American Academy of Pediatrics endorsed, recommends daily sleep by age (for example 10-13 hours at ages 3-5 and 9-12 hours at ages 6-12) and links getting enough sleep regularly with better attention, behavior, learning, memory and emotional regulation.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" />

In its 2016 statement on young children and media, the AAP recommended no screens other than video calls before 18 months; for parents who introduce media at 18-24 months, high-quality content used together; no more than 1 hour a day of high-quality programs for children over 2; and screen-free meals and no screens in the hour before bed.<Cite id="aap-2016-media" /> Its newest policy, from 2026, notes that most digital platforms are designed to boost engagement and profit, not children’s health and development.<Cite id="aap-2026-digital-ecosystems" />

<h3 id="school-age">School age: stay involved, and practice remembering</h3>

A meta-analysis of 448 studies found small positive links between parents’ involvement in school, such as talking about school and going to school events, and children’s achievement, engagement and motivation. The one exception was direct help with homework, which was linked with lower achievement.<Cite id="barger-2019" /> When parents were trained in how to support homework, though, elementary school children finished more of it and had fewer homework problems.<Cite id="patall-2008-homework" /> <Mixed />

For studying, a review of 10 study techniques rated practice testing and spreading practice out over time as high-utility techniques,<Cite id="dunlosky-2013" /> and an experiment found that even first graders remembered more words when a test was added to restudying.<Cite id="lipowski-2014" /> The pages for <L to="/learning/5-7-years/#homework">5-7 years</L> and <L to="/learning/7-10-years/#study">7-10 years</L> go into homework and study habits.

<h2 id="evidence-notes">How strong is the evidence?</h2>

A quick guide to which findings in this section are solid and which are still open.

**Fairly solid**

- Reading with young children is linked with language and early literacy, and programs that coach parents in sharing books give small gains in children’s language.<Cite id="bus-1995" /><Cite id="dowdall-2020" />
- Having choices raises motivation, effort and performance, and the boost to motivation is bigger for children than for adults.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" />
- Practicing recall helps memory more than restudying or other comparison activities, including in studies with preschoolers and first graders.<Cite id="fritz-2007" /><Cite id="lipowski-2014" /><Cite id="adesope-2017" />
- The sleep hours come from an expert consensus statement built on a review of 864 published articles.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" />

**Mixed or still open**

- Growth mindset programs: one report of two meta-analyses found both the link between mindset and school achievement and the effect of mindset programs weak overall,<Cite id="sisk-2018" /> a later meta-analysis found a small effect that was no longer significant after correcting for publication bias, or in the highest-quality studies,<Cite id="macnamara-burgoyne-2023" /> and another found benefits for some targeted groups when programs were delivered as designed, with wide variation expected between programs.<Cite id="burnette-2023" /> <Mixed />
- A national US experiment found that a short online mindset program raised grades for lower-achieving students, but it was done with high school students, not young children.<Cite id="yeager-2019" /> <Mixed />
- Praise: laboratory studies favor praising effort over intelligence, but a home study found no link between trait praise and later fixed beliefs.<Cite id="mueller-dweck-1998" /><Cite id="gunderson-2013" /> <Mixed />
- In a study of children with an average age of about 10, praise for personal qualities, but not for behavior, made children more likely to feel ashamed after failing, especially children with low self-esteem.<Cite id="brummelman-2014" /> <Mixed />
- Rewards: meta-analyses disagree on how widely rewards undermine children’s interest.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" /> <Mixed />
- Executive function training: gains appear, but lasting effects have not been convincingly shown.<Cite id="diamond-lee-2011" /><Cite id="takacs-kassai-2019" /> <Mixed />
- Dialogic reading and age: one meta-analysis found a smaller benefit at ages 4-5, another found child age made no difference to how much book-sharing programs helped.<Cite id="mol-2008-dialogic" /><Cite id="dowdall-2020" /> <Mixed />
- Homework help: one meta-analysis linked parents’ homework involvement with better results in elementary school, another linked direct homework help with lower achievement, and both links come from correlational studies.<Cite id="patall-2008-homework" /><Cite id="barger-2019" /> <Mixed />
- Much of the research on talk, number talk and screen time comes from single studies that observed families, so it shows links, not proof of cause.<Cite id="romeo-2018" /><Cite id="levine-2010" /><Cite id="madigan-2019-screens" /> <Mixed /> See <L to="/research/#correlation">correlation and causation</L>.
