<h2 id="why">Why this approach</h2>

Parent training has been tested so often that Mingebach et al. (2018) could pool 26 meta-analyses of it: for children under 13 treated for behavior problems, the effect on behavior was moderate and held up at follow-up.<Cite id="mingebach-2018" /> In 36 controlled trials with young children (average age 4.7), Comer et al. (2013) found the largest effects for behavioral treatments of disruptive behavior, and concluded that psychosocial treatments such as these should be the first-line treatment for early disruptive behavior problems.<Cite id="comer-2013" /> Two Cochrane reviews point the same way: group programs reduced conduct problems in 3- to 12-year-olds and improved parents' mental health,<Cite id="furlong-2012-cochrane" /> and there was evidence of fewer emotional and behavioral problems in children under 4.<Cite id="barlow-2016-cochrane" />

Effects grow with need: smallest when a program is offered to every family, largest when children are in treatment for clear problems.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> Do the gains last? Mingebach et al. (2018) saw them hold up at follow-up,<Cite id="mingebach-2018" /> while a meta-analysis by Lundahl et al. (2006) found only small effects by then,<Cite id="lundahl-2006" /> so keep the habits going.

What makes these programs work? Kaminski et al. (2008) went through 77 evaluations of programs for children up to age 7 and found larger effects when programs taught positive interaction and emotional communication, taught time-out and consistency, and had parents practice with their own child during sessions; teaching parents problem solving, or how to promote children's academic or social skills, went with smaller effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> Leijten et al. (2019) then checked 26 individual techniques, and only 3 were linked with stronger effects: positive reinforcement, praise in particular, and natural or logical consequences.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

The change can start with you. Forgatch and DeGarmo (1999) ran a trial with divorcing mothers of sons in grades 1 to 3, and the program helped the boys through their mothers' improved parenting, not directly.<Cite id="forgatch-degarmo-1999" /> The approach has also been tested with <L to="/approach/#chinese-families">Chinese families</L>,<Cite id="guo-2016-china" /><Cite id="leung-2003-hongkong" /> and school age is not too late: two large meta-analyses, one covering 156 trials, found no evidence that programs worked better when children started younger.<Cite id="gardner-2019-age" />

<h2 id="core">The five core skills</h2>

The five skills work as a set: 2 for the behavior you want, 2 for the behavior you don't, and 1 that holds it all together. This page starts with warm attention and adds consequences later. What each skill looks like at a given age is on the phase pages under <L to="/by-age/#phases">By age</L>.

<h3 id="attention">Warm attention and specific praise</h3>

**What to do.** Notice the behavior you want and respond warmly: a smile, a touch, a few words. Several times a day, say exactly what you liked (<L to="/toolbox/#specific-praise">specific praise</L>). If you can, add 5 to 10 minutes a day of one-on-one play where your child leads, with no questions, instructions or criticism (<L to="/toolbox/#connection-time">connection time</L>).

**Example.** Your child is drawing quietly while you cook. Walk over: “You've been drawing so calmly. I love the colors in that sky.”

<Say context="When you notice cooperation">You put your shoes away without being asked. Thank you!</Say>

<Say context="During one-on-one play">You're building a really tall tower. Now the red block goes on top.</Say>

**The evidence.** Programs that build positive interaction have larger effects,<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> and praise is one of the few techniques linked with stronger results.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> Leijten et al. (2018) found that adding relationship building to behavior management worked better than behavior management alone in treatment for behavior problems, worse in prevention programs, and no differently in effects lasting up to 3 years.<Cite id="leijten-2018" /> Praise does not buy obedience on the spot, though: on its own, it did not reliably increase compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /><Cite id="owen-2012" /> Nor does the wording matter as much as you might think: in two experiments with 3- to 9-year-olds, naming the behavior worked no better than a plain “Well done!”<Cite id="leijten-2016" />

<h3 id="instructions">Clear instructions</h3>

**What to do.** Give fewer instructions, and make each one count (<L to="/toolbox/#clear-expectations">clear expectations and instructions</L>). Get close and get your child's attention. Say one thing, as a statement, and say what to do rather than what to stop: “Feet on the floor, please,” not “Stop jumping!” Wait quietly for about 5 seconds. If your child starts, praise it; if not, give one warning and <L to="/approach/#follow-through">follow through</L>. For a task your child dislikes, use <L to="/toolbox/#when-then">when-then</L>.

**Example.** Instead of calling “Can you get ready?” from the kitchen, walk over, crouch down and say, “Time to put your shoes on.” Then wait.

<Say context="A clear instruction">Shoes on now, please.</Say>

<Say context="When-then">When the blocks are in the box, then you can have your snack.</Say>

**The evidence.** Radley and Dart (2016) reviewed 42 studies and concluded that delivering instructions effectively, and opening with a few easy requests before a harder one, may be considered evidence-based ways to increase cooperation.<Cite id="radley-dart-2016" /> In PCIT trials, observers saw children follow their parents' requests more than children in control groups did.<Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" />

<h3 id="ignoring">Planned ignoring of minor misbehavior</h3>

**What to do.** Some behavior is mostly a bid for attention: whining, fake crying, silly noises. Pick 1 or 2. When they happen, look away, stay calm and say nothing (<L to="/toolbox/#planned-ignoring">planned ignoring</L>). The moment the behavior stops, turn back warmly. If it gets louder at first, hold steady: giving in at that point is the one thing to avoid. Never ignore hitting, breaking things or anything dangerous.

**Example.** Your child whines, “I want juice nowww.” You keep folding laundry. When they ask in a normal voice, you turn right away: “Thanks for asking nicely. Sure.”

<Say context="Said once, before you start ignoring">I'll listen when you use your regular voice.</Say>

<Say context="The moment it stops">That's your regular voice. Thank you. What would you like?</Say>

**The evidence.** Briefly ignoring a child who has not cooperated reliably increased cooperation in pooled experiments that each tested a single parenting behavior.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> It works alongside plenty of attention for the behavior you want, not instead of it. More in <L to="/situations/#whining">Whining</L>.

<h3 id="consequences">Brief, calm consequences</h3>

**What to do.** For behavior you cannot ignore, such as hitting, breaking a rule you have explained, or refusing an important instruction after a warning, use a brief consequence, the same way every time:

- A <L to="/toolbox/#time-out">time-out</L>: a few minutes in a dull spot away from toys, screens and attention, then a fresh start with no lecture.
- A <L to="/toolbox/#privilege-removal">privilege removal</L>: losing something small for a short, set time, such as the tablet this evening.
- A <L to="/toolbox/#logical-consequences">logical consequence</L>: one tied to the behavior, such as a ball thrown indoors going on a shelf until after dinner.

Then go back to warm attention. With babies, move the baby or the object instead (see <L to="/by-age/0-12-months/#works">Babies</L>).

**Example.** Your child throws a block at their sister. “No throwing. Time-out.” A few minutes later: “Time-out is over.” No lecture.

<Say context="One warning">If you throw the ball inside again, it goes on the shelf until after dinner.</Say>

<Say context="Following through">You threw it again, so the ball goes on the shelf. You can have it after dinner.</Say>

**The evidence.** Programs that teach time-out and consistency have larger effects,<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> time-out for not cooperating reliably increased cooperation in experiments,<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> and natural and logical consequences are among the few techniques linked with stronger results.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> Some critics worry that time-out harms children's sense of security. Knight et al. (2020) followed families for years and found that using it at age 3 was not linked with any later difference in emotional, behavioral or relationship outcomes,<Cite id="knight-2020" /> and two reviews, Morawska and Sanders (2011) and Dadds and Tully (2019), conclude that careful use is justified and generally good for children.<Cite id="morawska-sanders-2011" /><Cite id="dadds-tully-2019" /> On length, see <L to="/little-time/#short-time-out">a short time-out</L>.

<h3 id="follow-through">Consistent follow-through</h3>

**What to do.** Do what you said you would do, calmly, every time. Give only instructions you will back up, one warning rather than a string of them, and let a time-out run its full length. Keep promises about good things too. It helps when every adult does the same (see <L to="/approach/#caregivers-disagree">when caregivers disagree</L>).

**Example.** “Two more slides, then we go home.” After the second slide, you go, even if there are tears. Stay kind and brief: “It's hard to leave. We'll come back on Saturday.”

<Say context="Keeping a when-then">Pajamas are on, so it's story time.</Say>

<Say context="When it is time to go">That was the last slide. You can walk with me, or I can carry you.</Say>

**The evidence.** Teaching consistency, together with time-out, is one of the ingredients linked with larger program effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> A small clinic study of time-out with preschoolers makes the same point from the other end: its authors concluded that enforcing a minimum time-out length was critical for teaching cooperation.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" />

<h2 id="getting-started">Getting started: the first weeks</h2>

This takes real effort: you are changing your own habits while tired and busy, and it will feel awkward for a while. A simple plan:

1. **Week 1: attention first.** Daily one-on-one play and lots of praise, with no new punishments. Note 2 or 3 behaviors you want more of and 1 or 2 you want less of, and tell the other adults who care for your child.
2. **Week 2: instructions and ignoring.** Fewer, clearer instructions, when-then, and ignoring one minor behavior.
3. **Week 3: one consequence.** Use it for 1 or 2 behaviors, such as hitting. Explain it at a calm time, practice it once (a teddy bear works), then use it every time.
4. **After that: keep going.** Keep up the play and praise even when things improve.

Practice matters. Programs in which parents practiced with their own child had larger effects,<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> and PCIT studies that required parents to reach a set skill level found bigger improvements.<Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" /> There is something in it for you as well: parents' mental health improved,<Cite id="furlong-2012-cochrane" /> and parenting stress went down.<Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" /> If nothing changes after several weeks of steady effort, see <L to="/approach/#not-working">when it is not working</L>.

<h2 id="programs">The four programs</h2>

You do not need to enroll in a program to use these skills, but the names tell you what to ask for.

- **PCIT (Parent-Child Interaction Therapy).** A therapist coaches the parent while parent and child play together. In 23 studies pooled together, it reduced children's behavior problems and parents' stress.<Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" />
- **Triple P (Positive Parenting Program).** A multi-level system, from light-touch media formats to more intensive group and individual programs. The pooled evidence runs to 101 studies with more than 16,000 families, and shows short- and long-term benefits for children and parents.<Cite id="sanders-2014" />
- **Incredible Years.** Often run with groups of parents. In 50 studies, it reduced disruptive behavior, most of all for children with more serious problems.<Cite id="menting-2013" />
- **PMTO (Parent Management Training, Oregon model).** Parents learn to encourage skills, set limits, supervise, solve problems and stay positively involved. It has been tested across settings, cultures and formats.<Cite id="forgatch-kjobli-2016" />

**What this means for you.** The core is shared, so you can start with this page. There is no clear evidence that programs lose their effect in ordinary services with non-specialist therapists,<Cite id="michelson-2013" /> and no single way of delivering them has been shown to be best,<Cite id="dretzke-2009" /> so choose what fits your family. Look for a program if behavior is severe, is not improving, or causes problems at school too. If money is tight, ask about one-on-one sessions: Lundahl et al. (2006) found that families facing economic hardship gained significantly more from individual than from group programs.<Cite id="lundahl-2006" />

<h2 id="chinese-families">Evidence from Chinese families</h2>

Does an approach developed in the West fit a Chinese family? It has been tested:

- **Shanghai.** Guo et al. (2016) randomized 81 parents, and those who took Group Triple P reported fewer child adjustment problems, better parenting, better well-being and more confidence than the wait-list group, plus more satisfaction with their children's academic achievement and fewer academic problem behaviors, all lasting to 6 months.<Cite id="guo-2016-china" />
- **Hong Kong.** A randomized trial here was similar in size, 91 parents of 3- to 7-year-olds with early conduct problems, and again the parents who took Triple P reported fewer child behavior problems, less dysfunctional parenting and more confidence than a wait-list group.<Cite id="leung-2003-hongkong" />
- **Hong Kong, PCIT.** Families of children with significant behavior problems who received PCIT (48 families) reported fewer behavior problems and less parenting stress than a comparison group (62 families). Observers saw more positive parenting, and the changes generally lasted 3 to 6 months.<Cite id="leung-2009-pcit" />
- **ADHD.** PCIT also reduced behavior and attention problems, parenting stress and negative parenting in a randomized trial with 64 Chinese children who had ADHD or ADHD features.<Cite id="leung-2017-pcit-adhd" />

These studies are smaller than the big Western reviews, and several rely on parents' own reports, so read them as encouraging rather than final. If praising out loud feels awkward because you grew up with little of it, start with one specific comment a day.

Parents often raise the next question themselves: plenty of children in China were hit and still did well at school, so why change? Inside China, children who are hit more show more acting out and weaker self-control as they grow, not less, in studies by Wang and Liu (2018) and Niu and Wang (2024).<Cite id="wang-liu-2018-reciprocal" /><Cite id="niu-wang-2024-ef" /> Among the Chinese American families Kim et al. (2013) followed, parents who were warm but also harsh had children with lower grades than parents who were warm without the harshness.<Cite id="kim-2013-tiger" /> The full answer, including what is still unknown, is on the physical discipline page: <L to="/physical-discipline/#school-success">children who were hit and still did well at school</L>.

<h2 id="edge-cases">Edge cases</h2>

Real life is messier than any plan. Here is how the same skills apply in common hard spots, and when to get professional help. Everyday problems such as tantrums, bedtime, screens and homework have their own guides in <L to="/situations/#intro">Situations</L>.

<h3 id="refuses-time-out">Your child refuses or leaves time-out</h3>

Stay calm and keep it boring.

1. Give one calm warning with a back-up (“If you get up again, no tablet tonight”), then follow through without arguing.
2. Use few words and a flat voice. Arguing or chasing turns time-out into attention.
3. Make going straight away pay off: when a small study shortened time-out for boys who went when told, 4 of the 6 became more likely to go when asked.<Cite id="donaldson-2013" />
4. Don't wait for silence. In two very small studies, both kinds of time-out worked: ones that simply ended on time, and ones that waited for the child to be quiet.<Cite id="donaldson-vollmer-2011" /><Cite id="mace-1986" />
5. Keep back-ups calm and never painful: walk or carry your child back, or use a barrier such as a safety gate or playpen where you can still see each other. Never lock a child in, hold them down or hit. Day and Roberts (1983), in a small clinic study of preschoolers who left time-out, found that a back-up without spanking (a “barrier” procedure) worked as well as spanking, and concluded that enforcing a minimum time-out length was the critical part.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" /> For the wider research debate, see <L to="/little-time/#what-studies-found">what the studies found</L>.

<Say context="When the time is up">Time-out is over. Let's try again: blocks in the box, please.</Say>

**Get help if** time-out turns into a daily battle, or you notice yourself getting rough. A therapist, for example in a program such as PCIT, can coach you through this moment.

<h3 id="aggression">Hitting and other aggression</h3>

Some hitting, biting and pushing is part of early childhood. Physical aggression shows up by 12 months, rises between ages 1 and 2, and declines from about the third birthday.<Cite id="alink-2006" /> Côté et al. (2006) followed a large group of Canadian children and found most were physically aggressive only rarely or almost never by the preteen years, while about 1 in 6 stayed at a high level.<Cite id="cote-2006" />

1. Stop it at once, calmly: step in, block, or separate the children.
2. Tend to the hurt child first.
3. Use the same brief consequence every time, such as time-out or losing the toy.
4. Later, practice what to do instead (say “Stop!”, walk away, or come and get you), and praise gentle play.

<Say context="Right away">No hitting. Hitting hurts. Time-out.</Say>

<Say context="Practicing later">If he grabs your truck, you can say ‘Stop, that's mine!’ and come and get me.</Say>

Behavioral treatments for young children, PCIT among them, reduce externalizing behavior, a research term that includes aggression.<Cite id="comer-2013" /><Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" /> Reynolds and Kelley (1997) showed the same logic working in a classroom with 4 aggressive preschoolers: teachers took away a smiley face, with a reprimand, for each aggressive act, children who kept at least one face chose a reward, and aggression dropped substantially.<Cite id="reynolds-kelley-1997" /> More in <L to="/situations/#hitting-biting">Hitting and biting</L>.

**Get help if** aggression is frequent or severe, causes injuries, happens at preschool or school too, or is not easing as your child gets older. Among the preschoolers in one study, those with disruptive behavior disorders were more often violent during tantrums, had more tantrums at school or daycare, and had a harder time recovering than healthy children.<Cite id="belden-2008" />

<h3 id="public-places">In public places</h3>

1. Before you go in, set the rule and the reward with a when-then.
2. Go when your child is fed and rested, and bring something to do.
3. Praise good behavior often while you are out.
4. Ignore minor fussing. For bigger problems, give one warning, then use a consequence you can carry out: a short time-out in a quiet corner, a few minutes in the car with you, or losing the promised treat.
5. If you need to leave, leave calmly. Consistency matters more than onlookers.

<Say context="Before going in">In the store, we walk next to the cart. When we're done, you can choose the apples.</Say>

The research here is about the core skills in general, not public places in particular. More in <L to="/situations/#public-meltdowns">Public meltdowns</L>.

**Get help if** outings are regularly impossible, or your child often runs off into danger (see <L to="/approach/#dangerous-behavior">dangerous behavior</L>).

<h3 id="siblings">Siblings</h3>

1. Catch them getting along: “You two are taking turns so nicely.”
2. Keep a few clear rules, such as no hitting and ask before taking. Hitting gets the usual consequence; if you didn't see who started it, both take a short break.
3. For everyday squabbles, coach rather than judge: each child says what happened and how they feel, then they suggest fixes.
4. Give each child some one-on-one time.

<Say context="Coaching a squabble">You both want the blue cup. Tell me what happened, one at a time. Then let's find a fix you both like.</Say>

Siddiqui and Ross (2004) trained mothers of 5- to 8-year-olds to mediate their children's disputes; compared with control families, the children reasoned and talked about feelings more often, and younger siblings especially became better able to solve conflicts, at least in the short term.<Cite id="siddiqui-ross-2004" /> Kennedy and Kramer (2008) worked from the other side, teaching 4- to 8-year-old siblings from 95 families emotional and social skills, and their relationships improved compared with families on a waiting list.<Cite id="kennedy-kramer-2008" /> Take sibling aggression seriously: children aged 0 to 9 who had experienced it in the past year showed more mental health distress.<Cite id="tucker-2013" /> More in <L to="/situations/#sibling-fighting">Sibling fighting</L>.

**Get help if** one child is often hurt, frightened or singled out.

<h3 id="caregivers-disagree">When caregivers disagree</h3>

1. Talk it over away from your child, when you are both calm.
2. Agree on 2 or 3 house rules and the consequence for each. A <L to="/printables/family-rules/">family rules poster</L> can help.
3. Don't overrule each other in front of your child unless something is unsafe; talk about it later.

<Say context="To the other adult, in the moment">Let's talk about this tonight.</Say>

Teubert and Pinquart (2010) pooled 59 studies and found cooperation, agreement and conflict between parents associated with children's adjustment; the effects were small, but they held after accounting for each parent's own parenting or for the quality of their marriage.<Cite id="teubert-pinquart-2010" />

**Get help if** disagreements turn into frequent fights, or one adult's discipline becomes harsh. A parenting program together, or couples counseling, is worth considering.

<h3 id="grandparents">When grandparents do the caregiving</h3>

1. Start with thanks, and ask rather than instruct.
2. Share the 2 or 3 things that matter most to you, such as praise for helping and the same response to hitting, and let smaller differences go.
3. Show rather than tell: let them see you praise and use time-out, or share this page.

<Say context="To a grandparent">Thank you for everything you do with them. We're trying something new: praising them right away when they do something well. Would you try it with us?</Say>

Kirby and Sanders (2014) ran a randomized trial with 54 grandparents caring for 2- to 9-year-olds: Grandparent Triple P reduced the behavior problems grandparents reported, raised their confidence, eased their stress, anxiety and depression, and improved their relationship with the child's parents, who also reported fewer behavior problems without attending.<Cite id="kirby-sanders-2014" /> More in <L to="/situations/#grandparents">Grandparents with different rules</L>.

**Get help if** disagreements cause lasting tension between the generations. A family session with a counselor is worth considering.

<h3 id="dangerous-behavior">Dangerous behavior</h3>

Running into the road, climbing onto a windowsill, reaching for the stove: act first, teach later.

1. Stop it physically and calmly: take the hand, pick your child up, move them away. Using your body to keep a child safe is right at any age (see <L to="/physical-discipline/#safety-holds">how to step in physically, calmly</L>).
2. Make it hard to happen again: close supervision, gates, locks, and dangerous things out of reach.
3. Set one clear rule with a consequence, and practice it, such as stopping at the curb together.
4. Never use planned ignoring for anything dangerous.

<Say context="In the parking lot">Hand, please. In the parking lot, we hold hands or ride in the cart.</Say>

Preschoolers with depression showed more self-harming behavior during tantrums than healthy children,<Cite id="belden-2008" /> so a young child who hurts themselves on purpose needs a professional's attention, not only discipline.

<Note kind="safety" title="Get help now">

If your child hurts themselves on purpose, hurts animals or plays with fire, or you cannot keep everyone safe, contact a doctor or mental health professional promptly. If someone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. Help lines are on the <L to="/about/#help-lines">About page</L>.

<h3 id="developmental-differences">ADHD, autism and other developmental differences</h3>

The core skills help children with ADHD and autism too, though results differ by outcome.

- **ADHD.** Daley et al. (2014) pooled 32 randomized trials of behavioral approaches for ADHD, and ratings by people who probably did not know which families had the program still showed better parenting and fewer conduct problems, though those blinded ratings did not show fewer ADHD symptoms.<Cite id="daley-2014" /> For preschoolers with ADHD or its symptoms, parents reported moderate improvements in symptoms and behavior lasting 3 to 12 months, but independent assessments confirmed only the drop in negative parenting.<Cite id="rimestad-2016" />
- **Autism.** Parent training reduced disruptive behavior in children with autism across 8 randomized trials, though results varied a lot between studies.<Cite id="postorino-2017" /> Bearss et al. (2015) randomized 180 children aged 3 to 7, and parent training reduced parent-reported disruptive behavior more than general autism education did, though how much that matters clinically is unclear.<Cite id="bearss-2015" />

Helpful adjustments: very short instructions, pictures and <L to="/toolbox/#routines">visual schedules</L>, quicker and more frequent praise, and small steps.

Ask your child's doctor about a developmental assessment if attention, activity or impulsiveness cause problems in more than one place, such as home and school; if you worry about how your child talks, plays or relates to others; or if your child loses words or skills.

In a 2020 clinical report, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended autism screening at 18 and 24 months and noted that autism can be diagnosed as young as 18 months.<Cite id="hyman-2020-autism" /> Revised US milestone checklists list skills that most children (75% or more) reach by each checkup age, so a missed milestone is a reason to ask, not a diagnosis.<Cite id="zubler-2022" /> For ADHD, the AAP's 2019 guideline added a recommendation on diagnosing and treating conditions that occur alongside it.<Cite id="wolraich-2019-adhd" />

You don't need to wait for an assessment to start using <L to="/approach/#core">the skills on this page</L>.

<h3 id="preteens">Preteens: more say, same core</h3>

From about 10, the approach stays the same but the balance shifts: more listening and negotiation, fewer orders, consequences agreed in advance. Pinquart (2017) pooled 1,435 studies and found warmth, clear behavioral limits and granting autonomy had small links with fewer behavior problems, while harsh control and psychological control (guilt, shaming, withdrawing love) had the strongest links with more.<Cite id="pinquart-2017-externalizing" /> Sixth and seventh graders who saw no growing part in family decisions turned more sharply toward peers,<Cite id="fuligni-eccles-1993" /> and Stattin and Kerr (2000), studying 14-year-olds, found that what parents knew about their children's lives came mainly from what the children chose to tell them, not from checking up.<Cite id="stattin-kerr-2000" /> Keep praise specific and private, swap time-out for short privilege removal, and take recurring fights to a weekly family meeting. The <L to="/by-age/10-12-years/#works">Preteens page</L> has the detail.

<h3 id="not-working">When it is not working after several weeks</h3>

Give a new plan a few weeks of steady effort before you judge it. Then check:

1. **Consistency.** Do you follow through every time, and do all the adults do the same?
2. **Positive attention.** Is there daily one-on-one time? Do you praise more than you correct? If you are not sure, count for an hour.
3. **Commands.** Count your instructions for an hour, too. Drop the unneeded ones, and make the rest clear.
4. **The consequence.** Is time-out really dull, with no chatting, toys or screens? Is the consequence small enough to use every time?
5. **Your own stress and support.** Exhaustion, low mood, money worries or conflict with a partner make every skill harder. Reyno and McGrath (2005) pooled parent-training studies and found results depended partly on things outside the child: low family income was the strongest predictor, and parents' education, mothers' mental health and how severe the problems were also mattered.<Cite id="reyno-mcgrath-2005" /> Support for you is part of the plan.
6. **Something else.** A new baby, a move, a new school, poor sleep or illness can all show up as behavior.

Ollendick et al. (2015) randomized children aged 7 to 14 with oppositional defiant disorder, and Collaborative & Proactive Solutions, in which adults and the child work out problems together, worked as well as parent management training.<Cite id="ollendick-2016" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#problem-solving">Solving problems together</L>.

**Get help if** things are no better after a month or two of steady effort, the behavior is severe or dangerous, problems also show up at school, or you feel out of control. Parent training helps most where problems are most serious,<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> so a program with a therapist is worth seeking when home efforts are not enough.

<h2 id="get-help">When to get professional help</h2>

Look for a parent-training program or a professional if:

- behavior problems are frequent and intense, last for months, or happen at preschool or school too;
- tantrums happen every day, or are very long, unpredictable or destructive (most preschoolers have tantrums sometimes, but only 8.6% had them daily in one large study);<Cite id="wakschlag-2012" />
- your child hurts other people, animals or themselves;
- you are worried about your child's development, attention or social skills;
- you feel close to losing control, or worry about how discipline is going at home (see <L to="/physical-discipline/#warning-signs">warning signs that discipline has become abuse</L>);
- your own mood, sleep or stress is getting in the way.

Start with your child's doctor, and ask for behavioral parent training such as <L to="/approach/#programs">PCIT, Triple P, Incredible Years or PMTO</L>, or a therapist who uses the same skills. A good sign: you practice with your own child and get feedback, not just listen to talks. Programs in which parents practiced with their own child during sessions had larger effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" />

If someone is in danger right now, call your local emergency number. Help lines are listed on the <L to="/about/#help-lines">About page</L>.
