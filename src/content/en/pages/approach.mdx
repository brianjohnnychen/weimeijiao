<h2 id="why">Why this approach</h2>

Parent training has been tested so often that one study pooled 26 meta-analyses of it: for children under 13 treated for behavior problems, the effect on behavior was moderate and held up at follow-up.<Cite id="mingebach-2018" /> In 36 controlled trials with young children (average age 4.7), behavioral treatments for disruptive behavior had the largest effects, and the authors concluded that non-medication treatments should come first.<Cite id="comer-2013" /> Cochrane reviews found that group programs reduced conduct problems in 3- to 12-year-olds and improved parents' mental health,<Cite id="furlong-2012-cochrane" /> and found evidence of fewer emotional and behavioral problems in children under 4.<Cite id="barlow-2016-cochrane" />

The effects grow with need: smallest when a program is offered to every family, largest when children are in treatment for clear problems.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> Some reviews found that gains held up over time,<Cite id="mingebach-2018" /> while another found small effects at follow-up for behavioral programs,<Cite id="lundahl-2006" /> so keep the habits going after things improve.

What makes these programs work? Across 77 evaluations of programs for children up to age 7, effects were larger when programs taught positive interaction and emotional communication, taught time-out and consistency, and had parents practice with their own child during sessions. Teaching parents problem solving, or how to promote children's academic or social skills, went with smaller effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> Of 26 techniques examined in another analysis, only 3 were linked with stronger effects: positive reinforcement, praise in particular, and natural or logical consequences.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

The change runs through you: in a trial with divorcing mothers of young sons, the program helped the boys through their mothers' improved parenting, not directly.<Cite id="forgatch-degarmo-1999" /> The approach has also been tested with <L to="/approach/#chinese-families">Chinese families</L>,<Cite id="guo-2016-china" /><Cite id="leung-2003-hongkong" /> and it is not too late if your child is already at school: two large meta-analyses, one covering 156 trials, found no evidence that programs worked better when children started younger.<Cite id="gardner-2019-age" />

<h2 id="core">The five core skills</h2>

The five skills work as a set: 2 for the behavior you want, 2 for the behavior you don't, and 1 that holds it all together. Like PCIT, this page starts with warm attention and adds consequences later.

<h3 id="attention">Warm attention and specific praise</h3>

**What to do.** Notice the behavior you want and respond warmly: a smile, a touch, a few words. Several times a day, say exactly what you liked (<L to="/toolbox/#specific-praise">specific praise</L>). If you can, add 5 to 10 minutes a day of one-on-one play where your child leads, with no questions, instructions or criticism (<L to="/toolbox/#connection-time">connection time</L>).

**Example.** Your child is drawing quietly while you cook. Walk over: “You've been drawing so calmly. I love the colors in that sky.”

<Say context="When you notice cooperation">You put your shoes away without being asked. Thank you!</Say>

<Say context="During one-on-one play">You're building a really tall tower. Now the red block goes on top.</Say>

**The evidence.** Programs that build positive parent-child interaction have larger effects,<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> and praise is one of the few techniques linked with stronger results.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> In treatment for behavior problems, adding relationship building to behavior management worked better than behavior management alone.<Cite id="leijten-2018" /> But praise does not buy obedience on the spot: on its own, it did not reliably increase compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /><Cite id="owen-2012" /> And wording matters less than you might think: in two experiments with 3- to 9-year-olds, naming the behavior worked no better than a plain “Well done!”<Cite id="leijten-2016" />

<h3 id="instructions">Clear instructions</h3>

**What to do.** Give fewer instructions, and make each one count (<L to="/toolbox/#clear-expectations">clear expectations and instructions</L>). Get close and get your child's attention. Say one thing, as a statement, and say what to do rather than what to stop: “Feet on the floor, please,” not “Stop jumping!” Wait quietly for about 5 seconds. If your child starts, praise it; if not, give one warning and <L to="/approach/#follow-through">follow through</L>. For a task your child dislikes, use <L to="/toolbox/#when-then">when-then</L>.

**Example.** Instead of calling “Can you get ready?” from the kitchen, walk over, crouch down and say, “Time to put your shoes on.” Then wait.

<Say context="A clear instruction">Shoes on now, please.</Say>

<Say context="When-then">When the blocks are in the box, then you can have your snack.</Say>

**The evidence.** A review of 42 studies concluded that delivering instructions effectively, and opening with a few easy requests before a harder one, may be considered evidence-based ways to increase cooperation.<Cite id="radley-dart-2016" /> In PCIT trials, observers saw children follow their parents' requests more than children in control groups did.<Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" />

<h3 id="ignoring">Planned ignoring of minor misbehavior</h3>

**What to do.** Some behavior is mostly a bid for attention: whining, fake crying, silly noises. Pick 1 or 2. When they happen, look away, stay calm and say nothing (<L to="/toolbox/#planned-ignoring">planned ignoring</L>). The moment the behavior stops, turn back warmly. If it gets louder at first, hold steady: giving in at that point is the one thing to avoid. Never ignore hitting, breaking things or anything dangerous.

**Example.** Your child whines, “I want juice nowww.” You keep folding laundry. When they ask in a normal voice, you turn right away: “Thanks for asking nicely. Sure.”

<Say context="Said once, before you start ignoring">I'll listen when you use your regular voice.</Say>

<Say context="The moment it stops">That's your regular voice. Thank you. What would you like?</Say>

**The evidence.** In experiments that tested single parenting behaviors, briefly ignoring a child after they did not cooperate reliably increased cooperation.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> Pair it with plenty of attention for the behavior you want. More in <L to="/situations/#whining">Whining</L>.

<h3 id="consequences">Brief, calm consequences</h3>

**What to do.** For behavior you cannot ignore, such as hitting, breaking a rule you have explained, or refusing an important instruction after a warning, use a brief consequence, the same way every time:

- A <L to="/toolbox/#time-out">time-out</L>: a few minutes in a dull spot away from toys, screens and attention, then a fresh start with no lecture.
- A <L to="/toolbox/#privilege-removal">privilege removal</L>: losing something small for a short, set time, such as the tablet this evening.
- A <L to="/toolbox/#logical-consequences">logical consequence</L>: one tied to the behavior, such as a ball thrown indoors going on a shelf until after dinner.

Then go back to warm attention. With babies, move the baby or the object instead (see <L to="/by-age/0-12-months/#works">Babies</L>).

**Example.** Your child throws a block at their sister. “No throwing. Time-out.” A few minutes later: “Time-out is over.” No lecture.

<Say context="One warning">If you throw the ball inside again, it goes on the shelf until after dinner.</Say>

<Say context="Following through">You threw it again, so the ball goes on the shelf. You can have it after dinner.</Say>

**The evidence.** Programs that teach time-out and consistency have larger effects,<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> time-out for not cooperating reliably increased cooperation in experiments,<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> and natural and logical consequences are among the few techniques linked with stronger results.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> Some critics argue that time-out harms children's sense of security. Yet in a long-term study, parents' use of time-out at age 3 was not linked with any difference in emotional, behavioral or parent-child relationship outcomes through fifth grade,<Cite id="knight-2020" /> and reviews conclude that careful use, the way these programs teach it, is justified and generally good for children's well-being.<Cite id="morawska-sanders-2011" /><Cite id="dadds-tully-2019" /> On length, see <L to="/little-time/#short-time-out">short time-outs</L>.

<h3 id="follow-through">Consistent follow-through</h3>

**What to do.** Do what you said you would do, calmly, every time. Give only instructions you are ready to back up, give one warning rather than a string of them, and let a time-out run its full length. Keep your promises about good things too. It helps when every adult does the same (see <L to="/approach/#caregivers-disagree">when caregivers disagree</L>).

**Example.** At the playground you say, “2 more slides, then we go home.” After the second slide, you go, even if there are tears. Stay kind and brief: “It's hard to leave. We'll come back on Saturday.”

<Say context="Keeping a when-then">Pajamas are on, so it's story time. You picked a good one.</Say>

<Say context="When it is time to go">That was the last slide. You can walk with me, or I can carry you.</Say>

**The evidence.** Teaching parents the importance of consistency is one of the ingredients linked with larger program effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> In a small clinic study of time-out with preschoolers, the researchers concluded that enforcing a minimum time-out length was critical for teaching cooperation.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" />

<h2 id="getting-started">Getting started: the first weeks</h2>

This takes real effort, especially at first. You are changing your own habits, often while tired and busy, and the skills will feel awkward for a while. A simple plan:

1. **Week 1: attention first.** Daily one-on-one play and lots of praise, with no new punishments yet. Note 2 or 3 behaviors you want more of and 1 or 2 you want less of, and tell the other adults who care for your child what you are doing.
2. **Week 2: instructions and ignoring.** Fewer, clearer instructions, when-then, and ignoring one minor behavior.
3. **Week 3: one consequence.** Use it for 1 or 2 behaviors, such as hitting. Explain it at a calm time, practice it once (a teddy bear works), then use it every time.
4. **After that: keep going.** Keep up the daily play and praise even when things get better.

Practice matters. Programs in which parents practiced with their own child had larger effects,<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> and PCIT studies that required parents to reach a set skill level before moving on found bigger improvements.<Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" /> It helps you, too: in reviews, parents' mental health improved,<Cite id="furlong-2012-cochrane" /> and parenting stress went down.<Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" /> If nothing has changed after several weeks of steady effort, see <L to="/approach/#not-working">when it is not working</L>.

<h2 id="programs">The four programs</h2>

You do not need to enroll in a program to use these skills, but the names tell you what to ask for if you want help.

- **PCIT (Parent-Child Interaction Therapy).** A therapist coaches the parent during play with the child, first in warm attention, then in instructions and time-out. In a meta-analysis of 23 studies, PCIT reduced children's behavior problems and parents' stress.<Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" />
- **Triple P (Positive Parenting Program).** A multi-level system, from light-touch media formats to more intensive group and individual programs. A meta-analysis of 101 studies with more than 16,000 families found short- and long-term benefits for children and parents.<Cite id="sanders-2014" />
- **Incredible Years.** Often run in parent groups that watch and discuss short videos of family situations. In a meta-analysis of 50 studies, it reduced disruptive behavior, more so for children with more serious problems.<Cite id="menting-2013" />
- **PMTO (Parent Management Training, Oregon model).** Parents learn to encourage skills, set limits, supervise, solve problems and stay positively involved. It has been tested across settings, cultures and formats.<Cite id="forgatch-kjobli-2016" />

**What this means for you.** The core is the same in all four, so you can start with this page. There is no clear evidence that programs lose their effect when run in ordinary services by non-specialist therapists,<Cite id="michelson-2013" /> and no single way of delivering them has been shown to be best,<Cite id="dretzke-2009" /> so choose what fits your family. Look for a program if your child's behavior is severe, is not improving with your own efforts, or causes problems at school too. If money is tight, ask about one-on-one sessions: in one meta-analysis, families facing economic hardship gained significantly more from individual than from group programs.<Cite id="lundahl-2006" />

<h2 id="chinese-families">Evidence from Chinese families</h2>

Some parents wonder whether an approach developed in the West fits a Chinese family. It has been tested:

- **Shanghai.** In a randomized trial with 81 parents, those who took Group Triple P reported fewer child adjustment problems, better parenting, better well-being of their own and more confidence than a wait-list group, plus more satisfaction with their children's academic achievement and fewer academic problem behaviors. The gains held at 6 months.<Cite id="guo-2016-china" />
- **Hong Kong.** In a randomized trial with 91 parents of 3- to 7-year-olds with early conduct problems, parents who took Triple P reported fewer child behavior problems, less dysfunctional parenting and more confidence as parents than a wait-list group.<Cite id="leung-2003-hongkong" />
- **Hong Kong, PCIT.** Families of children with significant behavior problems who received PCIT reported fewer behavior problems and less parenting stress than a comparison group. Observers saw more positive parenting, and the changes generally lasted 3 to 6 months.<Cite id="leung-2009-pcit" />
- **ADHD.** In a randomized trial with 64 Chinese children with ADHD or ADHD features, PCIT reduced behavior and attention problems, parenting stress and negative parenting.<Cite id="leung-2017-pcit-adhd" />

These studies are smaller than the big Western reviews, and several rely on parents' own reports, so read them as encouraging rather than final. If praising out loud feels awkward because you grew up with little of it, start with one specific comment a day.

<h2 id="edge-cases">Edge cases</h2>

Real life is messier than any plan. Here is how the same skills apply in common hard spots, and when to get professional help.

<h3 id="refuses-time-out">Your child refuses or leaves time-out</h3>

Stay calm and keep it boring.

1. Give one calm warning with a back-up (“If you get up again, no tablet tonight”), then follow through without arguing.
2. Use few words and a flat voice. Arguing, pleading or chasing turns time-out into attention.
3. Make going straight away pay off. In a small study, shortening time-out for boys who went when told helped 4 of the 6 go when asked.<Cite id="donaldson-2013" />
4. Don't wait for silence. In very small studies, time-outs that simply ended on time worked about as well as ones that waited for the child to be quiet.<Cite id="donaldson-vollmer-2011" /><Cite id="mace-1986" />
5. Keep back-ups calm and non-physical, and never lock a child in a room or hold them down. In a small clinic study of preschoolers who left time-out, a non-physical back-up (the authors call it a barrier procedure) worked as well as spanking, and enforcing the minimum time-out length was what mattered.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" /> For the wider research debate, see <L to="/little-time/#what-studies-found">what the studies found</L>.

<Say context="When the time is up">Time-out is over. Let's try again: blocks in the box, please.</Say>

**Get help if** time-out turns into a daily battle, or you notice yourself getting rough. PCIT therapists coach parents through exactly this moment.

<h3 id="aggression">Hitting and other aggression</h3>

Some hitting, biting and pushing is part of early childhood. Physical aggression shows up by 12 months, rises between ages 1 and 2, and declines from about the third birthday.<Cite id="alink-2006" /> In a large Canadian study, most children were physically aggressive only rarely or almost never by the preteen years, while about 1 in 6 stayed at a high level.<Cite id="cote-2006" />

1. Stop it at once, calmly: step in, block, or separate the children.
2. Tend to the hurt child first.
3. Use the same brief consequence every time, such as time-out or losing the toy.
4. Later, practice what to do instead (say “Stop!”, walk away, or come and get you), and praise gentle play when you see it.

<Say context="Right away">No hitting. Hitting hurts. Time-out.</Say>

<Say context="Practicing later">If he grabs your truck, you can say ‘Stop, that's mine!’ and come and get me.</Say>

Parent training reduces externalizing behavior, the research term that includes aggression.<Cite id="comer-2013" /><Cite id="thomas-2017-pcit" /> In a small classroom study, losing a smiley-face token after each aggressive act substantially reduced aggression in 4 preschoolers.<Cite id="reynolds-kelley-1997" /> More in <L to="/situations/#hitting-biting">Hitting and biting</L>.

**Get help if** aggression is frequent or severe, causes injuries, happens at preschool or school as well as at home, or is not easing as your child gets older. In one study, preschoolers with disruptive behavior disorders were more often violent during tantrums, had more tantrums at school or daycare, and had a harder time recovering than healthy children.<Cite id="belden-2008" />

<h3 id="public-places">In public places</h3>

1. Before you go in, set the rule and the reward with a when-then.
2. Go when your child is fed and rested, and bring something to do.
3. Praise good behavior often while you are out.
4. Ignore minor fussing. For bigger problems, give one warning, then use a consequence you can actually carry out: a short time-out in a quiet corner, a few minutes in the car with you, or losing the promised treat.
5. If you need to leave, leave calmly. Staying consistent matters more than what onlookers think.

<Say context="Before going in">In the shop, we walk next to the cart. When we're done, you can choose the apples.</Say>

The research here is about the core skills in general, not public places in particular. More in <L to="/situations/#public-meltdowns">Public meltdowns</L>.

**Get help if** outings are regularly impossible, or your child often runs off into danger (see <L to="/approach/#dangerous-behavior">dangerous behavior</L>).

<h3 id="siblings">Siblings</h3>

1. Catch them getting along and say so: “You two are taking turns so nicely.”
2. Keep a few clear rules, such as no hitting and ask before taking. Hitting gets the usual consequence; if you didn't see who started it, both take a short break.
3. For everyday squabbles, coach rather than judge: let each child say what happened and how they feel, then ask them for ideas.
4. Give each child some one-on-one time with you.

<Say context="Coaching a squabble">You both want the blue cup. Tell me what happened, one at a time. Then let's find a way you're both okay with.</Say>

When mothers of 5- to 8-year-olds were trained to mediate disputes this way, their children reasoned and talked about feelings more often than children in control families, and younger siblings especially became better able to solve conflicts, at least in the short term.<Cite id="siddiqui-ross-2004" /> A program that taught 4- to 8-year-old siblings emotional and social skills improved their relationships compared with a wait-list group, although the study was not randomized.<Cite id="kennedy-kramer-2008" /> Take sibling aggression seriously: in a national sample, children aged 0 to 9 who had experienced it in the past year showed more mental health distress.<Cite id="tucker-2013" /> More in <L to="/situations/#sibling-fighting">Sibling fighting</L>.

**Get help if** one child is often hurt, frightened or singled out.

<h3 id="caregivers-disagree">When caregivers disagree</h3>

1. Talk it over away from your child, when you are both calm.
2. Agree on 2 or 3 house rules and what happens when each one is broken. A <L to="/printables/family-rules/">family rules poster</L> can help.
3. Don't overrule each other in front of your child unless something is unsafe; talk about it afterwards.

<Say context="To the other adult, in the moment">Let's talk about this tonight.</Say>

How well caregivers work together is linked with how children do. In a meta-analysis of 59 studies, cooperation, agreement and conflict between parents were associated with children's adjustment; the effects were small, but they held after accounting for each parent's own parenting and the quality of their marriage.<Cite id="teubert-pinquart-2010" />

**Get help if** disagreements turn into frequent fights, or one adult's discipline becomes harsh. Doing a parenting program together, or couples counseling, is worth considering.

<h3 id="grandparents">When grandparents do the caregiving</h3>

1. Start with thanks, and ask rather than instruct.
2. Share the 2 or 3 things that matter most to you, such as praise for helping and the same response to hitting, and let smaller differences go.
3. Show rather than tell: let them see you praise and use time-out, or share this page with them.

<Say context="To a grandparent">Thank you for everything you do with them. We're trying something new: praising them right away when they do something well. Would you try it with us?</Say>

There is a program designed for grandparents. In a randomized trial with 54 grandparents caring for 2- to 9-year-olds, Grandparent Triple P reduced the behavior problems grandparents reported, raised their confidence, eased their own stress, anxiety and depression, and improved their relationship with the child's parents, who also reported fewer behavior problems without attending.<Cite id="kirby-sanders-2014" /> More in <L to="/situations/#grandparents">Grandparents with different rules</L>.

**Get help if** disagreements cause lasting tension between the generations. A family session with a counselor is worth considering.

<h3 id="dangerous-behavior">Dangerous behavior</h3>

Running into the road, climbing onto a windowsill, reaching for the stove: act first, teach later.

1. Stop it physically and calmly: take the hand, pick your child up, move them away. Using your body to keep a child safe is right at any age (see <L to="/physical-discipline/#safety-holds">safety holds</L>).
2. Make it hard to happen again: close supervision, gates, locks, and dangerous things out of reach.
3. Set one clear rule with a consequence, and practice it: stop at the curb together, then praise.
4. Never use planned ignoring for anything dangerous.

<Say context="In the parking lot">Hand, please. In the parking lot, we hold hands or ride in the cart.</Say>

Preschoolers with depression showed more self-harming behavior during tantrums than healthy children,<Cite id="belden-2008" /> so a young child who hurts themselves on purpose needs a professional's attention, not only discipline.

If your child hurts themselves on purpose, hurts animals or plays with fire, or if you cannot keep them or others safe, contact a doctor or mental health professional promptly. <h3 id="developmental-differences">ADHD, autism and other developmental differences</h3>

The core skills help children with ADHD and autism too, though the results differ by outcome.

- **ADHD.** In a meta-analysis of 32 randomized trials of behavioral approaches for ADHD, ratings by people who probably did not know which families had the program still showed better parenting and fewer conduct problems, but there was no blinded evidence that ADHD symptoms themselves went down.<Cite id="daley-2014" /> For preschoolers with ADHD or its symptoms, parents reported moderate improvements in symptoms and behavior that lasted 3 to 12 months, but independent assessments confirmed only the drop in negative parenting.<Cite id="rimestad-2016" />
- **Autism.** A meta-analysis of 8 randomized trials found that parent training reduced disruptive behavior in children with autism, although results varied a lot between studies.<Cite id="postorino-2017" /> In a trial with 180 children aged 3 to 7, parent training reduced parent-reported disruptive behavior more than general autism education did, though how much that matters clinically is unclear.<Cite id="bearss-2015" />

Helpful adjustments: very short instructions, pictures and <L to="/toolbox/#routines">visual schedules</L>, quicker and more frequent praise, and tasks broken into small steps.

Ask your child's doctor about a developmental assessment if attention, activity level or impulsiveness cause problems in more than one place (such as at home and at school); if you are worried about how your child talks, plays or relates to others; if your child loses words or skills they used to have; or if behavior stays severe despite several weeks of steady effort.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends standard autism screening at 18 and 24 months along with ongoing developmental checks, and notes that autism can be diagnosed as young as 18 months.<Cite id="hyman-2020-autism" /> Revised US milestone checklists list skills that most children (75% or more) reach by each checkup age, so a missed milestone is a reason to ask, not a diagnosis.<Cite id="zubler-2022" /> For ADHD, the AAP's 2019 guideline added a recommendation on diagnosing and treating conditions that occur alongside it.<Cite id="wolraich-2019-adhd" /> You do not have to wait for discipline to fail: an assessment and parent training can happen at the same time.

<h3 id="not-working">When it is not working after several weeks</h3>

Give a new plan a few weeks of steady effort before you judge it. If it still is not working, check:

1. **Consistency.** Do you follow through every time? Do all the adults do the same?
2. **Enough positive attention.** Is there daily one-on-one time? Do you praise more than you correct? If you are not sure, count for an hour.
3. **Too many commands.** Count your instructions for an hour, too. Drop the ones that are not needed, and make the rest clear.
4. **The consequence itself.** Is time-out really dull, or does it come with chatting, toys or screens? Is the consequence small enough to use every time?
5. **Your own stress and support.** Exhaustion, low mood, money worries or conflict with a partner make every skill harder. In a meta-analysis, how well parent training worked depended partly on things outside the child: low family income was the strongest predictor, and parents' education, mothers' mental health and the severity of the child's problems also mattered.<Cite id="reyno-mcgrath-2005" /> Support for you is part of the plan.
6. **Something else going on.** A new baby, a move, a new school, poor sleep or a health problem can all show up as behavior.

For children 7 and older with oppositional defiant disorder, there is another option: in a randomized trial, Collaborative & Proactive Solutions, in which adults and the child work out problems together, worked as well as parent management training.<Cite id="ollendick-2016" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#problem-solving">Solving problems together</L>.

**Get help if** things are no better after a month or two of steady effort, the behavior is severe or dangerous, problems also show up at school, or you feel angry or out of control. Parent training helps most where problems are most serious,<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> so a program with a therapist is worth seeking when home efforts are not enough.

<h2 id="get-help">When to get professional help</h2>

Look for a parent-training program or a professional if:

- behavior problems are frequent and intense, last for months, or happen at preschool or school as well as at home;
- tantrums happen every day, or are very long, unpredictable or destructive (most preschoolers have tantrums sometimes, but only 8.6% had them daily in one large study);<Cite id="wakschlag-2012" />
- your child hurts other people, animals or themselves;
- you are worried about your child's development, attention or social skills;
- you feel close to losing control, or you worry about how discipline is going at home (see <L to="/physical-discipline/#warning-signs">warning signs</L>);
- your own mood, sleep or stress is getting in the way.

Your child's doctor is a good first stop. Ask specifically about behavioral parent training, such as PCIT, Triple P, Incredible Years or PMTO, or about a therapist who uses the same core skills. A good sign: you practice with your own child and get feedback, rather than only listening to talks. Programs built that way had larger effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" />

If someone is in danger right now, call your local emergency number. Help lines are listed on the <L to="/about/#help-lines">About page</L>.
