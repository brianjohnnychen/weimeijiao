<h2 id="intro">Ages are a guide, not a deadline</h2>

Children move through these phases at their own pace, and the phases overlap. A 3-year-old can act like a toddler in the morning and like a preschooler by lunch. Even the milestone checklists that pediatricians use list what most children (75% or more) can do by a given age, which means some children will get there later.<Cite id="zubler-2022" />

So start with the page for your child's age, and read the one before or after it if that fits better. If you are not sure, the 10-question <L to="/printables/age-finder/">age finder</L> suggests a phase based on what your child does now. If something about your child's development worries you, talk to your pediatrician.

<h2 id="how-to-use">How to use these pages</h2>

Each phase page opens with a short "Start here" list for when you need something right now. Then come four sections: what's normal at this age, what works and why, what backfires, and things you can say, with scripts written for that age. Each phase also has a <L to="/printables/#summaries">one-page summary</L> you can print and put on the fridge.

The method underneath all five phases is the same: warm attention for the behavior you want, clear instructions, ignoring minor misbehavior, brief and calm consequences, and steady follow-through. It is explained once, with its evidence, in <L to="/approach/#core">the core of the best-proven approach</L>. The phase pages show how it changes shape as your child grows, from redirecting a baby to holding family meetings with a 9-year-old.

If your child is older than you would like, you have not missed your chance. Two meta-analyses of parenting program trials found no evidence that starting earlier in childhood made the programs work better for disruptive behavior.<Cite id="gardner-2019-age" />
