<h2 id="safety-vs-punishment">Keeping a child safe is not punishment</h2>

Two different things often get called physical discipline.

- **Physical intervention for safety:** grabbing a child who runs toward the street, holding a child who is about to hurt someone, carrying a child away from danger. This is appropriate and necessary at every age.
- **Physical punishment:** spanking, smacking, slapping or hitting with an object, used to correct or deter behavior through pain or discomfort. This is the contested topic the rest of this page covers.

The difference is purpose and timing. A safety hold stops harm in the moment and ends as soon as everyone is safe. Physical punishment comes after the behavior, as a penalty.

<h3 id="safety-holds">How to step in physically, calmly</h3>

1. **Act fast, then slow down.** Grab first if you have to. Once the danger has passed, slow your movements and your voice.
2. **Use only the force you need.** Hold a hand, an arm or the body firmly enough to stop the danger, without squeezing, twisting, yanking or shaking.
3. **Say what you are doing in a few words**, in a low, steady voice.
4. **To stop hitting or kicking**, step between the children, hold your child's hands, or pick up a small child and move away. Never press on the chest, neck or back, never hold a child face down, and never shake.
5. **Let go as soon as everyone is safe.**
6. **Afterwards, keep it short:** name the danger, state the rule and reconnect.

<Say context="Holding a child back from the road">I'm holding you so you don't run into the road.</Say>

<Say context="Stopping a hit">I won't let you hit. I'm holding your hands to keep everyone safe.</Say>

<Say context="Afterwards">Cars can hurt you. In the parking lot, we hold hands.</Say>

With babies and toddlers, safety-proof the home so you need fewer emergency grabs. If you find yourself holding or restraining your child often, talk to a professional; see <L to="/approach/#get-help">when to get help</L>.

<h2 id="majority-view">What most research finds</h2>

The larger body of research, summarized in several meta-analyses and reviews, links physical punishment with worse outcomes for children.

- **Spanking meta-analyses.** Pooling 111 effect sizes from 160,927 children in meta-analyses focused specifically on spanking, Gershoff and Grogan-Kaylor (2016) found that 13 of 17 mean effects were significantly different from zero, all of them linking spanking with a higher risk of detrimental outcomes; the size of these links did not differ substantially between spanking and physical abuse, or by study design.<Cite id="gershoff-grogan-kaylor-2016" />
- **Corporal punishment meta-analysis.** In an earlier meta-analysis, Gershoff (2002) found corporal punishment associated with all 11 of the child behaviors and experiences examined, including higher immediate compliance, but also more aggression, lower moral internalization (taking rules on as one's own) and worse mental health.<Cite id="gershoff-2002" />
- **Over time.** Heilmann et al. (2021) reviewed 69 prospective studies and found that physical punishment consistently predicted increases in behavior problems, was not associated with positive outcomes over time, was linked with a higher risk of involvement with child protective services, and predicted worsening behavior in quasi-experimental studies; the links held across child and parent characteristics, with some evidence that more physical punishment went with worse outcomes.<Cite id="heilmann-2021" />
- **Babies.** Among the 2,788 urban families Lee et al. (2014) studied, 30% of 1-year-olds had been spanked at least once in the past month, and spanking at age 1 was linked with a 33% greater probability of child protective services involvement between ages 1 and 5.<Cite id="lee-2014-cps" />
- **Across settings.** Weighing this research against accepted criteria for judging cause, Gershoff et al. (2018) concluded that physical punishment is linked with the same harms as physical abuse, and that its links with detrimental outcomes are consistent across cultural, family and neighborhood contexts; they recommend that parents avoid it.<Cite id="gershoff-2018-causal" />
- **Shouting and shaming.** In its 2018 policy statement, the American Academy of Pediatrics links harsh verbal abuse with mental health problems in preteens and adolescents.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> Wang and Kenny (2014) followed 976 two-parent families: harsh verbal discipline at age 13 predicted increases in conduct problems and depressive symptoms over the following year, parental warmth did not soften these links, and teenagers' misbehavior also predicted more harsh verbal discipline, so the links ran both ways.<Cite id="wang-kenny-2014" /> Both concern older children; we include them because shouting can look like the obvious replacement for spanking.

**How strong is the case for cause?** Most of these studies are observational: they follow or survey families rather than assigning discipline at random, so on their own they show links, not proof of cause. Majority-side researchers have weighed the research against accepted criteria for causal inference and, on that basis, recommend that parents avoid physical punishment.<Cite id="gershoff-2018-causal" /> Quasi-experimental studies, which come closer to testing cause, also found that physical punishment predicted worsening behavior.<Cite id="heilmann-2021" /> The minority side disputes this reading, as the next section explains. For more on reading this kind of evidence, see <L to="/research/#correlation">correlation and causation</L>.

<h2 id="minority-view">What proponents argue</h2>

A smaller group of researchers argues that the case against all spanking is overstated. These are their main points, in their terms.

**The links shrink when earlier behavior is controlled.** Larzelere et al. (2024) pooled controlled longitudinal studies and found that customary spanking explained less than 1% of the remaining variance in each child outcome once children's earlier adjustment was controlled; they attribute harmful-looking results to residual confounding (differences the studies did not fully account for, such as how much a child was already misbehaving), point to large effects of spanking used to enforce time-out in clinic programs for defiant young children, and discourage blanket injunctions against spanking.<Cite id="larzelere-2024" /> Ferguson (2013), pooling longitudinal studies, found small but non-trivial links between spanking or corporal punishment and later behavior problems and lower cognitive performance; once children's earlier levels were controlled, the links with behavior problems were statistically significant but trivial, and the review concluded that the impact was minimal.<Cite id="ferguson-2013" />

**Other methods look harmful too, with the same analyses.** When data on 785 children aged 6 to 9 were re-analyzed with the original study's simple three-category measure of earlier behavior, spanking appeared to increase antisocial behavior. Grounding and psychotherapy showed a similar pattern, as did, in part, privilege removal and sending children to their room. With fuller measures of earlier behavior problems, all of these effects, spanking included, became non-significant. Larzelere et al. (2010), who ran the re-analysis, argue that how and when a method is used may matter more than which method it is.<Cite id="larzelere-2010-bmc" /> Larzelere (2000) had made a similar point in a review: apparently detrimental outcomes have been found for every alternative disciplinary tactic when studied with similar analyses.<Cite id="larzelere-2000-review" />

**"Conditional" spanking.** Larzelere and Kuhn (2005), comparing physical punishment with other discipline tactics across 26 studies in a meta-analysis, report that what they call conditional spanking was favored over 10 of 13 alternative tactics for reducing noncompliance or antisocial behavior, that customary physical punishment did as well as the alternatives (apart from one large study that favored physical punishment), and that only overly severe or predominant use compared unfavorably.<Cite id="larzelere-kuhn-2005" /> Larzelere's 2000 review reported beneficial outcomes, such as less noncompliance and fighting, mainly when nonabusive spanking was used to back up milder discipline with 2- to 6-year-olds, and attributed detrimental outcomes in longitudinal studies chiefly to overly frequent use.<Cite id="larzelere-2000-review" />

As proponents describe it, then, the conditions under which they report benefits are narrow: nonabusive spanking, with children of about 2 to 6, used as a back-up to milder discipline such as time-out. Overly frequent, severe or predominant use is where they, too, report worse outcomes. This is their position, set out so you can weigh it. It is not this site's advice, and this site gives no instructions for physical punishment.

**A critique of the 2002 meta-analysis.** Baumrind et al. (2002), with Larzelere among the authors, argued in a commentary that its biases and confounds limit causal conclusions, that corporal punishment may be linked with poor outcomes because it marks inept, harsh parenting, and that although the harm of physical abuse and other extreme punishment is clear, the evidence it presented did not justify a blanket injunction against spanking.<Cite id="baumrind-2002" />

**Culture and family autonomy.** Parents also raise arguments that are not about statistics:

- In our culture it is normal, and children understand it as care.
- I was spanked as a child and I turned out fine.
- How we discipline is our family's decision.

Research speaks to some of these. Lansford et al. (2005) studied mothers and children in 6 countries, including China, and found physical discipline less strongly linked with poor child outcomes where it was seen as more normal, which supports part of the cultural argument; but it was still linked with more adverse outcomes however normal it was seen to be, and in every country, more physical discipline went with more aggression and anxiety.<Cite id="lansford-2005" /> In the same 6 countries, Gershoff et al. (2010) linked corporal punishment with more aggression and anxiety in 8- to 12-year-olds, with some moderation by how normal children thought it was, and also linked yelling and expressing disappointment with more aggression, and time-out and shaming with more anxiety, so the picture is not simple for any method.<Cite id="gershoff-2010-international" /> Both are cross-sectional studies, which show links rather than causes.

On "I turned out fine": research describes average risks across many children. It cannot say how any one person would have turned out with different discipline. On family choice, the minority researchers above argue on evidence grounds that a blanket injunction against spanking is not justified.<Cite id="baumrind-2002" /> In its 2018 policy statement, the American Academy of Pediatrics, looking at the same field, recommends against all corporal punishment.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" />

Positive parenting programs have also been tested with Chinese families: parents in Shanghai and Hong Kong who took Triple P in randomized trials reported fewer child adjustment or behavior problems than parents on a wait list.<Cite id="guo-2016-china" /><Cite id="leung-2003-hongkong" /> See <L to="/approach/#chinese-families">Evidence from Chinese families</L>.

<h2 id="agreement">Where both sides agree</h2>

Read side by side, the two camps agree on more than the headlines suggest.

- **Harsh, frequent or severe physical punishment is harmful.** Proponents attribute detrimental findings chiefly to overly frequent use.<Cite id="larzelere-2000-review" /> They report that overly severe or predominant use compares unfavorably with other discipline,<Cite id="larzelere-kuhn-2005" /> and they call the harm of physical abuse and other extreme punishment clear.<Cite id="baumrind-2002" /> Majority researchers find some evidence that more physical punishment goes with worse outcomes.<Cite id="heilmann-2021" />
- **Non-physical discipline works.** Even in the proponents' meta-analysis, customary physical punishment did no better than alternative tactics, apart from one large study.<Cite id="larzelere-kuhn-2005" /> The benefits proponents report come mainly from spanking used as a back-up to milder discipline,<Cite id="larzelere-2000-review" /> and in a direct test, a non-physical back-up for time-out worked as well as spanking.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" /> In experiments, time-out and briefly ignoring a child after noncompliance robustly increased compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" />
- **The wider pattern of parenting matters.** Proponents argue that how and when a method is used may matter more than which method it is,<Cite id="larzelere-2010-bmc" /> and that spanking may look harmful because it marks inept, harsh parenting.<Cite id="baumrind-2002" /> Parenting-program research finds larger effects for programs that build positive parent-child interaction and consistency.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" />

<h2 id="disagreement">Where they disagree</h2>

The real disagreement is narrower than it looks. It is mainly about ordinary, occasional spanking: whether it adds harm, and whether it adds anything as a back-up.

- **Does ordinary spanking add harm?** The majority finds consistent links with detrimental outcomes, robust across child and parent characteristics, and of similar size to the links for physical abuse.<Cite id="gershoff-grogan-kaylor-2016" /><Cite id="heilmann-2021" /> The minority finds that once children's earlier behavior is controlled, the links become trivial, or explain less than 1% of the remaining variance.<Cite id="ferguson-2013" /><Cite id="larzelere-2024" />
- **Cause or confounding?** Majority researchers have weighed the evidence against accepted criteria for cause and recommend that parents avoid physical punishment.<Cite id="gershoff-2018-causal" /> Minority researchers argue that biases and confounds limit causal conclusions, and attribute harmful-looking results to residual confounding.<Cite id="baumrind-2002" /><Cite id="larzelere-2024" />
- **Does a spanking back-up for time-out help?** Proponents point to large effects in clinic programs that used spanking to enforce time-out.<Cite id="larzelere-2024" /> In a direct comparison, a non-physical "barrier" back-up worked as well as spanking.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" />
- **What should parents be told?** In its 2018 policy statement, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends against all corporal punishment.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> The minority argues that the evidence does not justify blanket injunctions against spanking.<Cite id="baumrind-2002" /><Cite id="larzelere-2024" />

One more point: no peer-reviewed source on this site shows that physical discipline is faster or more effective for busy parents. For that question in detail, see <L to="/little-time/#physical-discipline">the little-time guide</L>.

<h2 id="major-bodies">What medical bodies recommend</h2>

This site cites only policy statements published in peer-reviewed journals. On physical punishment, that is the 2018 policy statement of the American Academy of Pediatrics, published in *Pediatrics*.

- It advises parents and other adults not to use any corporal punishment, including hitting and spanking, and not to use verbal abuse such as threatening, insulting, humiliating or shaming.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" />
- It states that such aversive strategies are minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term, and links corporal punishment with an increased risk of negative behavioral, cognitive, psychosocial and emotional outcomes.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" />
- In their place, it recommends positive reinforcement of appropriate behavior, limit setting, redirecting and setting future expectations.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" />

Researchers on the minority side disagree with blanket recommendations of this kind, as described under <L to="/physical-discipline/#disagreement">where they disagree</L>.

<h2 id="decide">Questions to ask yourself</h2>

There is no score at the end. These questions are meant to help you decide with the evidence in view.

- **What do I want my child to learn from this moment, and will this teach it?**
- **Am I calm, or am I reacting?** If you are angry, wait until you are calm before deciding anything.
- **Have I given non-physical methods a fair try?** That means a few weeks of using them the same way every time, not a few days. See <L to="/approach/#not-working">when it is not working</L>.
- **Is it becoming more frequent, or harder?** Both sides see frequent or severe use as harmful; see <L to="/physical-discipline/#agreement">where both sides agree</L>.
- **If I am weighing the proponents' view, does what happens in my home fit the conditions they describe?** Nonabusive, with a child of about 2 to 6, as a back-up to milder discipline, and not frequent, severe or the main method.
- **Do the other adults who care for my child agree?** See <L to="/approach/#caregivers-disagree">when caregivers disagree</L>.
- **How does my child react afterwards?** Calmer and closer, or fearful, secretive or more defiant?
- **When my child is angry with someone smaller, what do I want them to do?**

<h2 id="warning-signs">Warning signs that discipline has become abuse</h2>

Reach out for help now if any of these are true:

- **You hit with an object**, such as a belt, stick, ruler, hanger or slipper. Zolotor et al. (2008) surveyed mothers anonymously in 2 US states: those who reported spanking with an object had markedly higher odds of also reporting physical abuse.<Cite id="zolotor-2008" />
- **It is happening more often, or harder.** The same survey linked more frequent spanking with higher odds of reported abuse.<Cite id="zolotor-2008" /> Across prospective studies, too, there is some evidence that more physical punishment goes with worse outcomes.<Cite id="heilmann-2021" />
- **You hit a baby or toddler.** Spanking at age 1 was linked with a higher chance of child protective services involvement by age 5, and Lee et al. (2014) advise professionals to counsel parents not to spank infants and toddlers.<Cite id="lee-2014-cps" />
- **You shake your child, or hit the head or face.**
- **It leaves marks, bruises or injuries.**
- **You hit in a rage, or find it hard to stop once you start.**
- **Your child seems afraid of you**: flinching when you move quickly, hiding, or lying to avoid being hit.
- **You hide it from other adults**, or other adults have told you they are worried.
- **Threats, insults, humiliation or shaming have become routine.** The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against all of these in its 2018 policy statement.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" />

<Note kind="safety" title="Never shake a baby or child">

Never shake a baby or young child, for any reason. If crying is pushing you to the edge, put the baby down on their back somewhere safe, such as a crib, step away for a few minutes, and call someone. The American Academy of Pediatrics has a policy statement devoted to abusive head trauma in infants and children, which asks pediatricians to stay alert to its signs.<Cite id="narang-2020" />

</Note>

If any of these apply, talk to your pediatrician or family doctor, or use the help lines on the <L to="/about/#help-lines">About page</L>. If your child is hurt, get medical care. If you fear you might hurt your child, make sure your child is somewhere safe, step away and call someone. Parenting programs are one kind of help: in the Cochrane review by Furlong et al. (2012), group-based behavioral parenting programs reduced conduct problems in children aged 3 to 12 and also improved parents' mental health.<Cite id="furlong-2012-cochrane" /> See also <L to="/approach/#get-help">when to get professional help</L>.

<h2 id="instead">What to do instead, in the moment</h2>

1. **Stop any danger first**, with a calm <L to="/physical-discipline/#safety-holds">safety hold</L> if needed.
2. **Pause yourself.** Hands down, step back, breathe out slowly. If your child is safe, take a minute in another room.
3. **Say very little.** One short sentence is enough (see the script below).
4. **Once you are calm, use one brief non-physical consequence:** a <L to="/toolbox/#time-out">short time-out</L> or <L to="/toolbox/#privilege-removal">losing a privilege</L>; for a toddler, <L to="/toolbox/#redirection">redirection</L>; for whining and other bids for attention, <L to="/toolbox/#planned-ignoring">planned ignoring</L>.
5. **Then return to normal.** No grudges. Look for the next chance to notice something your child does well.

<Say context="When you feel the urge to hit">Stop. I'm going to calm down, then we'll work this out.</Say>

Leijten et al. (2018) pooled experiments in which time-out and briefly ignoring a child after noncompliance robustly increased compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> Day and Roberts (1983) compared the two back-ups for leaving time-out directly, and the non-physical one worked as well as spanking.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" />

<h2 id="repair">After you lose your temper</h2>

If you shouted or hit in anger, you can still repair it with your child.

1. **Calm down first.**
2. **Check on your child.** Make sure they are not hurt.
3. **Name what happened**, without excuses or "but you...".
4. **Say sorry, and say what you will do next time.**
5. **Reconnect**: a hug if your child wants one, or a few minutes together.
6. **Keep the limit.** The original rule still stands.
7. **Plan for next time.** Notice your own early warning signs, such as a raised voice or a tight jaw, and decide now what you will do instead.

<Say context="After hitting or shouting">I hit you when I was angry. I'm sorry. Next time I feel that angry, I'll step away and calm down first.</Say>

<Say context="Keeping the limit">The rule hasn't changed: we don't throw toys. Let's pick them up together.</Say>

Tronick (1989), writing about babies and their caregivers rather than about discipline, suggests that healthy development is linked with interactions in which mismatches are frequently repaired, and poorer development with long stretches of failed interaction and negative feeling.<Cite id="tronick-1989" /> A parent losing their temper is a much bigger rupture than those everyday mismatches, so take this as a guiding idea, not proof that repair undoes harm. More on how to repair: <L to="/toolbox/#repair">Repair after conflict</L>.

If you lose your temper often, treat it as a sign to get support for yourself; see the <L to="/physical-discipline/#warning-signs">warning signs</L>.
