<h2 id="talk">Talk: keep the conversation going</h2>

Toddlers’ words arrive at their own pace. What you can do is keep the conversation going: listen, answer, add a little, and wait. When your toddler says “Truck!”, you might say “A big truck! It’s going so fast.”

Studies that observed families at home point the same way, though each is a single study: more back-and-forth turns at 18-24 months were linked with stronger language and IQ scores 10 years later,<Cite id="gilkerson-2018" /> and parents’ use of varied, less common words with toddlers was linked with larger vocabularies a year later, beyond how much the parents talked.<Cite id="rowe-2012" /> <Mixed />

- Expand on what your toddler says, adding a word or two.
- Use real, specific words: digger, ladle, whisper, enormous.
- Ask a question, then wait a few seconds for the answer.

<Say context="Your toddler points and says “Dog!”">Yes, a dog! A little brown dog. He’s wagging his tail. Do you think he’s happy?</Say>

<h2 id="read">Read: let your toddler talk too</h2>

Dialogic reading turns a picture book into a conversation: you ask questions, build on your toddler’s answers, and slowly hand over more of the telling. An early version was tested in a 1-month home program in which parents asked more open-ended questions and questions about what things are like and what they do, expanded on their child’s answers and did less straight reading.<Cite id="whitehurst-1988" /> A meta-analysis of 16 studies later found that dialogic reading gave a moderate boost to the words children can say, compared with ordinary shared reading.<Cite id="mol-2008-dialogic" />

The same meta-analysis found the boost much smaller for 4- and 5-year-olds and for children at risk of language and literacy problems,<Cite id="mol-2008-dialogic" /> while a later meta-analysis of 19 trials found child age unrelated to how much book-sharing programs helped.<Cite id="dowdall-2020" /> <Mixed /> Either way, the toddler years are a fine time to start.

Programs that coach parents in sharing books had a large effect on parents’ skill, but very short programs did little for children’s language.<Cite id="dowdall-2020" /> It is a skill you build over time.

- Ask “what” questions: What’s that? What color is it?
- Ask what things do: What does a cow say? What do we do with a spoon?
- Repeat and expand the answer: “A cow! Yes, a black and white cow.”

<Say context="Looking at a farm book together">What’s this? Yes, a cow! What does a cow say? Moo! The cow is eating grass.</Say>

<h2 id="play">Play: follow, then add a little</h2>

The American Academy of Pediatrics describes play with parents and other children as a singular opportunity to build social, emotional, thinking, language and self-control skills.<Cite id="yogman-2018-play" /> A good approach with toddlers is to follow their lead and add just a little.

In guided play, your child leads and you gently steer toward something to learn. In a meta-analysis of studies with children averaging 1 to 8 years old, guided play did better than direct teaching on early math, shapes and switching between tasks, and better than free play on words for space and position, but no differently on other key outcomes.<Cite id="skene-2022-guided-play" /> <Mixed />

- Join in on the floor and copy what your toddler is doing before adding an idea.
- Name shapes, sizes and positions as you play: on top, under, next to, bigger.
- Offer a small challenge: “Can you find another block like this one?”

<Say context="Building a tower">You put the big block at the bottom. Where should the little one go?</Say>

<h2 id="praise">Praise: notice the effort</h2>

One study filmed 53 families at home and followed the children for years. More praise for toddlers’ effort was linked with children believing, at 7-8 years old, that ability can grow, enjoying challenges and looking for ways to improve,<Cite id="gunderson-2013" /> and in a follow-up of the same children, more praise for effort and strategies at ages 1-3 was linked with better math and reading in fourth grade, through those beliefs.<Cite id="gunderson-2018" /> <Mixed />

That is one small group of families, and a link is not proof of cause. The same study also found that praising traits, such as “You’re so smart”, was not linked with fixed beliefs later on.<Cite id="gunderson-2013" /> <Mixed /> So there is no need to watch every word. A good habit is simply to say what your toddler did.

- Describe the effort: “You kept trying.”
- Describe the strategy: “You turned it around and it fit.”

For praise as part of everyday discipline, see <L to="/toolbox/#specific-praise">Specific praise and attention</L>.

<Say context="Your toddler finally fits a puzzle piece">You turned it and turned it until it fit!</Say>

<h2 id="motivation">Motivation: let them do it</h2>

“Me do it!” is a big part of being a toddler, and that drive is worth protecting. Choices help: across 41 studies, having a choice raised intrinsic motivation, effort and performance, the effect was stronger for children than for adults, and it was stronger with 2 to 4 choices in a row and when no reward followed.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" />

Letting toddlers lead is not the same as having no limits. A review draws that line clearly, and reports that in observational studies, parents’ support for autonomy is linked with toddlers taking rules and values on as their own.<Cite id="joussemet-2008" />

Go easy on rewards for things your toddler already enjoys. Two meta-analyses disagree about how widely rewards harm motivation,<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" /> <Mixed /> but both report that tangible rewards promised in advance for an interesting task can reduce later interest in it.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" />

- Offer two choices you are happy with, rather than an open question.
- Leave time for “me do it” when you are not in a rush.

For more, see <L to="/toolbox/#choices">Offering choices</L>.

<Say context="Getting dressed">Do you want the red socks or the striped socks?</Say>

<h2 id="numbers">Numbers: count what you can see</h2>

In one study of 44 children, parents varied widely in how much they talked about numbers with their toddlers between 14 and 30 months, and more number talk was linked with a better grasp, at 46 months, of what number words mean (that “four” means a group of four things).<Cite id="levine-2010" /> <Mixed /> Another study found that counting or naming how many things the child could see was the kind of number talk linked with this understanding, especially for groups of 4 to 10.<Cite id="gunderson-levine-2011" /> <Mixed />

Across 64 studies, a richer home math environment was linked with higher math achievement, with an average correlation of r = .13 and large differences between studies.<Cite id="daucourt-2021" />

- Count things as you go: stairs, grapes, socks, cars.
- Point to each one as you count, then say the total: “One, two, three. Three cars!”
- Count bigger groups too, up to 10.

<Say context="Snack time">Let’s count your grapes. One, two, three, four, five. Five grapes!</Say>

<h2 id="sleep-screens">Sleep and screens</h2>

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 11-14 hours of sleep per 24 hours at ages 1-2 and 10-13 hours at ages 3-5, naps included, in a statement the American Academy of Pediatrics endorsed.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" /> Sleep matters for focus too: a review notes that lack of sleep impairs the executive functions behind self-control and working memory.<Cite id="diamond-2013" />

The AAP’s 2016 statement on young children and media recommended no screens other than video calls before 18 months; at 18-24 months, high-quality programs or apps used together, because that is how toddlers learn best; for children over 2, no more than 1 hour a day of high-quality programs, shared with a parent; and screen-free meals with no screens in the hour before bed.<Cite id="aap-2016-media" />

In a study of 2,441 Canadian children, more screen time at 2 and 3 years was linked with lower scores on a developmental screening test at 3 and 5, with both screen time and development reported by mothers.<Cite id="madigan-2019-screens" /> <Mixed /> The AAP’s newest policy, from 2026, also notes that most digital platforms are designed to boost engagement and profit, not children’s health and development.<Cite id="aap-2026-digital-ecosystems" />

For everyday screen struggles, see <L to="/situations/#screens">Screens</L>; for bedtime, see <L to="/situations/#bedtime">Bedtime battles</L>.

<h2 id="evidence">How sure is this?</h2>

- **Fairly solid:** sharing books, asking questions as you read and offering choices are backed by meta-analyses.<Cite id="mol-2008-dialogic" /><Cite id="dowdall-2020" /><Cite id="patall-2008-choice" />
- **Expert guidance:** the sleep hours and screen limits are recommendations from expert bodies.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" /><Cite id="aap-2016-media" />
- **Promising but limited:** the talk, praise and number-talk findings each come from single studies that observed families, so they show links, not proof of cause.<Cite id="gilkerson-2018" /><Cite id="gunderson-2013" /><Cite id="levine-2010" /> <Mixed /> See <L to="/research/#correlation">correlation and causation</L>.
- **Debated:** how widely rewards undermine motivation.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" /> <Mixed />
- **Narrow benefits:** guided play beat other approaches only on early math, shapes, task switching and words for space.<Cite id="skene-2022-guided-play" /> <Mixed />
