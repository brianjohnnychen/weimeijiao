<h2 id="talk">Talk: answer, then wait</h2>

Babies are learning language long before they say a word. Think of it as serve and return: your baby serves (a coo, a look, a reach), you return it (a smile, a word, a touch), and then you wait for the next serve.

Babies whose parents respond to them tend to do better. A review of infant research reports that parents’ responsiveness to their babies’ exploring and communicating predicts word learning,<Cite id="tamis-lemonda-2014" /> and in a randomized trial with mothers of full-term and very low birth weight babies, 10 home visits on responding to the baby made mothers more responsive than a comparison group between 6 and 13 months, which helped the babies’ social, emotional, communication and thinking skills grow.<Cite id="landry-2006" />

The same review suggests why: a reply that comes right away and fits what your baby is doing helps them link words to things, and shows them that language is a way to share what they mean.<Cite id="tamis-lemonda-2014" />

Speak to your baby, not just near them. In one study of Spanish-speaking families with low socioeconomic status, babies who heard more speech addressed to them processed familiar words more efficiently and could say more words by age 2, while talk they only overheard was not linked with their vocabulary.<Cite id="weisleder-fernald-2013" /> <Mixed />

- Get face to face, close enough to see each other’s expressions.
- Name what your baby looks at or reaches for.
- Copy their sounds, then wait. A few seconds can feel long, but it gives your baby a turn.
- Narrate ordinary routines: “Now the left sock. Now the right sock.”

<h2 id="read">Books: look, point, name</h2>

With a baby, reading mostly means looking, pointing and naming. Let your baby hold the book, pat the pictures and turn the pages out of order. When their eyes land on something, name it and add a little: “A duck! The duck says quack.”

Most reading research starts a little later. A meta-analysis of 19 trials that coached parents to share picture books involved children whose average ages were 1 to 6, and found small gains in children’s language and a large gain in parents’ book-sharing skills.<Cite id="dowdall-2020" /> So there is little trial evidence for the first year itself. Still, books give you endless things to talk about, and a few minutes a day is an easy habit to start now.

- Choose sturdy board or cloth books with clear pictures and faces.
- Keep it short, and stop when your baby loses interest.
- Songs and nursery rhymes count too.

<h2 id="play">Play: follow your baby’s lead</h2>

Play is how babies explore their world. The American Academy of Pediatrics describes play with parents as a singular opportunity to build social, emotional, thinking, language and self-regulation skills, and as a way to build the safe, stable, nurturing relationships that children need to thrive.<Cite id="yogman-2018-play" />

Let your baby choose what to explore. A review of parenting research reports that, in observational studies, parents’ support for their child’s autonomy is linked with babies’ motivation.<Cite id="joussemet-2008" /> At this age that can be as simple as letting your baby decide which toy to grab, and joining in rather than taking over.

You will not read every signal right, and that is normal. A review of research on babies’ emotional communication suggests that healthy development may go with interactions in which mix-ups happen often and get repaired, while long stretches of failed connection and distress go with poorer development.<Cite id="tronick-1989" /> Missed cues are part of every relationship; what you can do is notice and reconnect.

- Floor time on the tummy and on the back, with a few safe objects: cups, spoons, soft balls.
- Peekaboo, hiding a toy under a cloth, rolling a ball back and forth.
- Check that everything within reach is too big to swallow.

For a daily slot of this kind of play, see <L to="/toolbox/#connection-time">Connection time</L>.

<h2 id="sleep-screens">Sleep and screens</h2>

For babies 4-12 months, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 12-16 hours of sleep per 24 hours, naps included. Its statement, endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, starts at 4 months and links getting enough sleep regularly with better attention, behavior, learning, memory and emotional regulation.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" /> For bedtime routines and night waking, see <L to="/toolbox/#routines">Routines and visual schedules</L>, and <L to="/situations/#bedtime">Bedtime battles</L>.

In its 2016 statement on young children and media, the AAP recommended avoiding screen media other than video calls before 18 months.<Cite id="aap-2016-media" /> So video calls with grandparents, or with a parent who is away, are fine under that advice.

Live people seem to matter for early speech learning: in one study, American 9-month-olds who had 12 lab sessions with Mandarin speakers in person held on to their ability to hear Mandarin speech sounds, while babies who heard the same Mandarin from video or audio recordings did not.<Cite id="kuhl-2003" /> <Mixed />

<h2 id="evidence">How sure is this?</h2>

- **Fairly solid:** responding to your baby supports language and development, backed by a review and a randomized trial.<Cite id="tamis-lemonda-2014" /><Cite id="landry-2006" />
- **Expert guidance:** the sleep hours and the screen advice are recommendations from expert bodies, not the result of a single trial.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" /><Cite id="aap-2016-media" />
- **Limited:** the findings on speech aimed at babies and on live versus recorded language each come from one study.<Cite id="weisleder-fernald-2013" /><Cite id="kuhl-2003" /> <Mixed />
- **Little studied:** the shared-reading trials in one meta-analysis involved children with average ages of 1 to 6, so the first year is not well covered.<Cite id="dowdall-2020" />
- **Correlation, not cause:** studies that observe families can show links but not prove cause. <L to="/research/#correlation">Correlation and causation</L> explains why.
