<h2 id="read">Reading together</h2>

Learning to read is slow work. Keep reading aloud even after your child can read a little: you take the harder books that match their interests, and they read you a sentence or a page.

A meta-analysis of 99 studies, from preschool to university, found that children and students who read more in their free time tended to have better reading comprehension and spelling, and weaker readers seemed to benefit too.<Cite id="mol-bus-2011" /> These studies show a link, not proof of cause. Summer break is a good time to keep reading going: in 41 summer reading programs from kindergarten to grade 8, both classroom lessons and home programs built around children's own book reading improved reading, and the programs helped children from low-income families more.<Cite id="kim-quinn-2013" />

- Let your child pick some of the books, including comics and books that look easy.
- Talk about the story: what might happen next, how a character feels.

<h2 id="numbers">Numbers and math</h2>

Simple board games are an easy way in. In one study, low-income preschoolers (average age 5.4) who played a board game with numbered squares in a row for roughly 1 hour got better at comparing numbers, placing numbers on a number line, counting and reading numerals, and the gains were still there 9 weeks later. Classmates who played the same game with colored squares instead of numbers did not improve.<Cite id="ramani-siegler-2008" /> <Mixed />

More broadly, a meta-analysis of 64 studies found that children whose home math environment was richer (math activities, but also attitudes, expectations and talk about math at home) tended to have higher math achievement (r = .13), with large differences between studies.<Cite id="daucourt-2021" />

- Count stairs, share snacks out fairly, compare prices at the store.
- Cook together and let your child do the measuring.
- Play dice games, dominoes and card games such as "higher or lower".

<h2 id="homework">First homework</h2>

Whether your child gets homework at this age depends on the school. If they do, aim for a calm habit, not perfect pages. In a review of US homework research, correlational studies found a weaker link between homework and achievement in kindergarten to grade 6 than in grades 7-12, and all the studies had design flaws.<Cite id="cooper-2006-homework" />

The kind of help matters. When parents of elementary school children were trained in how to support homework, children completed more of it, had fewer homework problems and possibly did better at school.<Cite id="patall-2008-homework" /> Across 448 studies, most kinds of parent involvement, such as talking about school or going to school events, had small positive links with children's achievement, engagement and motivation, varying little with age. The one exception was homework help, which went with lower achievement.<Cite id="barger-2019" /> That is a correlation: it cannot show whether help lowers achievement or whether children who are struggling simply get more help.

- Same time and place each day, after a snack.
- Sit nearby, help your child get started, then step back.
- When they are stuck, ask what they have tried before you explain.
- If homework often ends in tears or takes much longer than the teacher expects, talk to the teacher.

<h2 id="study">Remembering what they learn</h2>

Quizzing helps even young children remember. In an experiment with first and third graders learning lists of words, adding a test to restudying led to better memory than restudying alone, even for first graders. Only the third graders realized that the testing had helped.<Cite id="lipowski-2014" /> So at this age it is up to you to turn practice into a game.

Spacing practice out helps too. In two experiments with preschool children learning names for toys, practice that asked children to recall the names at gradually longer gaps doubled what they remembered, and about half of the benefit came from the spacing itself. Offering a reward did not significantly improve learning.<Cite id="fritz-2007" />

- Cover the word or the sum, ask your child to say it, then check together.
- For spelling words or new characters, have your child write them from memory, then check.
- Do a few minutes on several days rather than one long session.

<h2 id="praise">Praise that helps</h2>

Describe what your child did: the effort, the strategy, the checking, the not giving up. In two small role-play studies with 5- and 6-year-olds, children who received person feedback (praise or criticism of themselves or their traits) reacted more helplessly to a later setback, including blaming themselves, than children who received feedback about the process.<Cite id="kamins-dweck-1999" /> <Mixed /> In a study that observed 53 families at home, parents' praise for effort at ages 1-3 predicted children's belief at 7-8 that ability can grow, while praise for inborn traits was not linked to later beliefs that ability is fixed.<Cite id="gunderson-2013" /> <Mixed />

So describing the process is the safer habit. The evidence that praising traits does harm comes mainly from short experiments.

<Say>You checked every answer. That was careful work.</Say>

Growth mindset programs have shown weak effects on achievement overall (see <L to="/learning/7-10-years/#growth-mindset">growth mindset</L>).<Cite id="sisk-2018" /> <Mixed /> For everyday praise, see <L to="/toolbox/#specific-praise">Specific praise and attention</L>.

<h2 id="motivation">Motivation and rewards</h2>

Choices help. A meta-analysis of 41 studies found that being given choices raised intrinsic motivation, effort and performance, and the boost to motivation was stronger for children than for adults and when people made a few (2 to 4) choices in a row.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" /> Let your child choose which book to read or which task to do first.

Rewards need more care. In a classic experiment, children who already liked an activity and agreed to do it for a promised reward later showed less interest in it than children who got a surprise reward or none.<Cite id="lepper-1973" /> Two meta-analyses disagree about how widespread that harm is.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" /> <Mixed /> Both found harm from promised, tangible rewards for tasks people already found interesting, and positive effects from verbal praise, though one found that tangible rewards tended to do more harm, and praise less good, for children than for college students, and the other found that rewards tied to the level of performance did not lower motivation.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" />

In practice: no stickers or money for reading, drawing or anything else your child already enjoys. Warm, specific feedback is fine. See also <L to="/toolbox/#choices">Offering choices</L>.

<h2 id="focus">Focus and self-control</h2>

Waiting for a turn, keeping instructions in mind and switching from play to work rely on what psychologists call executive functions. A review names three core ones (self-control, working memory and mental flexibility) and notes that stress, lack of sleep, loneliness and lack of exercise each impair them.<Cite id="diamond-2013" />

Many activities can strengthen them. A review of programs for children aged 4-12 found gains from computer training, games, aerobic exercise, martial arts, yoga, mindfulness and some school curricula. The successful ones all involved repeated practice that grew harder over time, and children with weaker skills gained most.<Cite id="diamond-lee-2011" /> <Mixed /> A later series of meta-analyses (children up to 12) found that these skills can be fostered, but found no convincing evidence that the gains last; approaches that build them indirectly worked as well as or better than drills, were more enjoyable and fit more easily into everyday life.<Cite id="takacs-kassai-2019" /> <Mixed />

- Play games that ask for waiting and switching: Simon says, "red light, green light", memory cards.
- Pick activities your child enjoys that take regular practice and slowly get harder, such as a sport, dance, music or martial arts. You do not need brain-training apps.

<h2 id="sleep-screens">Sleep and screens</h2>

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine, in a statement endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), recommends 10-13 hours of sleep in every 24 hours for children aged 3-5 and 9-12 hours for ages 6-12, and links getting enough sleep regularly with better attention, behavior, learning, memory and emotional regulation.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" />

The AAP's 2016 statement on young children and media, which covers ages 0 to 5, recommended screen-free meals and no screens in the hour before bed, and, for children aged 2 to 5, no more than 1 hour a day of high-quality programs, shared with a parent.<Cite id="aap-2016-media" /> In a systematic review of 67 studies of school-aged children and teenagers, 90% linked screen time with worse sleep, mostly shorter sleep and later bedtimes, though cause and effect was not confirmed.<Cite id="hale-guan-2015" />

- Keep the same bedtime on school nights, with a short routine: bath, teeth, a book, lights out.
- Watch together when you can, and talk about what you see.
