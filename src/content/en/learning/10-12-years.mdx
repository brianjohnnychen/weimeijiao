<h2 id="homework">Homework</h2>

Homework starts to count for more. A review of the US research found the link between homework and achievement stronger in grades 7 to 12 than in kindergarten to grade 6, though every study had design flaws.<Cite id="cooper-2006-homework" /> The kind of involvement matters. Parents setting rules about homework had a stronger link with achievement than other kinds of involvement, in one research synthesis.<Cite id="patall-2008-homework" /> Hands-on help is different. Across 448 studies, nearly every kind of involvement had small positive links with achievement, engagement and motivation, but homework help went with lower achievement.<Cite id="barger-2019" /> The same exception turned up when Hill and Tyson (2009) pooled 50 middle school studies, now close to your child's age: involvement helped, except help with homework.<Cite id="hill-tyson-2009" /> <Mixed /> These are <L to="/research/#correlation">correlations</L>, so they cannot say whether help lowers achievement or whether struggling children get more help.

- Hand the routine over: your child picks the time and place within your limits and sets it up.
- Be available for a question, then step back. Ask what they have tried before you give a hint.
- Check that it is done and handed in, not that every answer is right.
- If homework regularly takes far longer than the school expects, talk to the teacher together.

See also <L to="/situations/#homework-now">Homework</L>, and <L to="/toolbox/#routines">Routines and visual schedules</L>.

<h2 id="study">Studying that sticks</h2>

Preteens start to study for tests, and the methods that feel productive are often the weakest. Rereading a passage looked better on a test 5 minutes later and felt more confident, in two experiments, but the students who had practiced recalling it instead remembered much more after 2 days or 1 week.<Cite id="roediger-karpicke-2006" /> Practice tests beat restudying and every other comparison in one meta-analysis, with the size of the benefit varying with the learners and the study.<Cite id="adesope-2017" /> In the 50 classroom experiments one systematic review gathered, most showed medium or large benefits, though few took place outside Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic countries.<Cite id="agarwal-2021" /> Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated 10 study techniques and judged practice testing and spaced practice highly useful because they help learners of different ages and abilities,<Cite id="dunlosky-2013" /> and the best gap between study sessions grows as the time until the test grows, across 317 experiments.<Cite id="cepeda-2006" />

- Flashcards or a covered page: answer from memory, check, sort into "know it" and "not yet".
- Three short sessions on different days beat one long one the night before.
- After a chapter, close the book and write the five main points from memory.

<h2 id="read">Reading for pleasure</h2>

Reading for pleasure often drops off at this age, and it is worth protecting. Free-time reading had moderate to strong links with comprehension, reading and spelling from preschool to university across 99 studies; print exposure explained 13% of the variance in oral language skills in elementary school and 19% in middle school, and weaker readers seemed to benefit too. Mol and Bus (2011) describe an upward spiral, but the studies show links, not proof of cause.<Cite id="mol-bus-2011" />

- Let your child choose: series, comics, nonfiction, manga, audiobooks.
- Keep a family reading time rather than a rule about pages.
- Do not pay per book (see <L to="/learning/10-12-years/#motivation">motivation</L>).

<h2 id="praise">Praise and mindset</h2>

The strongest praise research is on exactly this age. Mueller and Dweck (1998) ran six studies with fifth graders, and the children praised for intelligence rather than effort cared more about performing than learning and, after a failure, persisted less, enjoyed the task less and did worse.<Cite id="mueller-dweck-1998" /> Among the 313 children, average age 10.4, whom Brummelman et al. (2014) studied, praise for personal qualities, but not for what they did, made children more likely to feel ashamed after failure, especially those with low self-esteem.<Cite id="brummelman-2014" /> <Mixed /> Praise the strategy, the effort and the progress.

<Say>You changed your approach halfway through and it worked. That's the skill.</Say>

<h3 id="growth-mindset">What about growth mindset?</h3>

Two large meta-analyses found the effects of growth mindset programs on achievement weak overall, and in the later one the small average effect was no longer significant after correcting for possible publication bias or in the highest-quality studies.<Cite id="sisk-2018" /><Cite id="macnamara-burgoyne-2023" /> <Mixed /> Another meta-analysis found effects (d = 0.14) for targeted groups when programs were delivered as designed, and Yeager et al. (2019), in a national US experiment in secondary schools, found that a short online program raised lower achievers' grades.<Cite id="burnette-2023" /><Cite id="yeager-2019" /> <Mixed /> Process praise is a sensible daily habit. A mindset lesson on its own is not a lever. For everyday praise, see <L to="/toolbox/#specific-praise">Specific praise and attention</L>.

<h2 id="motivation">Motivation and a say in the work</h2>

Across 36 studies, parents' support for their child's autonomy was linked with higher achievement and with children's own motivation, engagement, positive attitudes toward school and, most strongly, psychological health.<Cite id="vasquez-2016" /> Autonomy support is not permissiveness.<Cite id="joussemet-2008" /> Choice itself helps: in 41 studies, being given choices raised intrinsic motivation, effort and performance, and the boost was stronger for children than for adults.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" /> And there is a reason it matters now: Eccles et al. (1993) argue that part of the dip in motivation at the move to secondary school comes from a mismatch between early adolescents' growing need for a say and schools and homes that give them less of one.<Cite id="eccles-1993-stage-environment" />

Rewards are debated. Across 128 studies, rewards for doing, finishing or doing well at a task lowered later free-choice interest, with tangible rewards tending to be more harmful for children than for college students.<Cite id="deci-1999" /> A rival meta-analysis concluded that rewards are generally not harmful, and that rewards tied to the level of performance did not lower motivation.<Cite id="cameron-2001" /> <Mixed /> Whether money for grades backfires is therefore disputed; what both found was harm from promised, tangible rewards for tasks that were already interesting.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" />

In practice: let your child decide the order of subjects, the study method and the time within your limits; explain the rules you keep; do not pay for reading or for hobbies they already love. More in <L to="/toolbox/#choices">Offering choices</L> and <L to="/toolbox/#family-meetings">Family meetings</L>.

<h2 id="focus">Focus, planning and self-control</h2>

Planning a project and leaving the phone alone both draw on the three core executive functions, self-control, working memory and mental flexibility, which stress, lack of sleep, loneliness and lack of exercise each impair.<Cite id="diamond-2013" /> The programs for children aged 4-12 that improved these skills, from martial arts and aerobic exercise to games, mindfulness and school curricula, all involved repeated practice that grew harder over time.<Cite id="diamond-lee-2011" /> <Mixed /> A series of meta-analyses (children up to 12) found no convincing evidence that training gains last, and found that approaches that build these skills indirectly worked as well as or better than drills; the authors note they are more enjoyable for children.<Cite id="takacs-kassai-2019" /> <Mixed /> The stakes are long-term: when Moffitt et al. (2011) followed a cohort from birth to 32, childhood self-control predicted adult health, finances and offending along a gradient, a link rather than proof of cause.<Cite id="moffitt-2011" />

- Planning is the new skill: a weekly planner, big projects broken into dated steps, a checklist for the school bag.
- A sport, an instrument or chess: activities that take practice and get harder.
- Phones out of sight during homework; one task at a time.

<h2 id="sleep-screens">Sleep, screens and social media</h2>

The recommendation for ages 6 to 12 is still 9-12 hours of sleep in every 24 hours, from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine in a statement the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) endorsed, which links getting enough sleep regularly with better attention, behavior, learning, memory and emotional regulation.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" /> Preteens start sleeping less, but Carskadon (2011) concludes that sleep need does not decline through the teenage years even though sleep does; the loss comes from a mix of biological, psychological and social pressures.<Cite id="carskadon-2011" /> Cain and Gradisar (2010) reviewed 36 studies of school-aged children and adolescents and found later bedtimes and shorter sleep the outcomes most consistently related to electronic media use,<Cite id="cain-gradisar-2010" /> and a systematic review of 67 studies advises limiting screens especially before and during bedtime hours.<Cite id="hale-guan-2015" />

For ages 5 to 18, the AAP's 2016 policy statement asks families to make a Family Media Use Plan with consistent rules, boundaries on content and on sharing personal information, mentoring and open conversation, and says the evidence does not support a one-size-fits-all approach.<Cite id="aap-2016-school-aged-media" /> Its 2026 statement adds that most platforms are designed to boost engagement and profit, not to support children's health and development.<Cite id="aap-2026-digital-ecosystems" /> On well-being itself, the best large studies find only small associations with technology use, explaining at most 0.4% of the variation in the three large datasets Orben and Przybylski (2019) analyzed, and a review judged the most rigorous findings unlikely to be of practical significance and unable to separate cause from effect.<Cite id="orben-przybylski-2019" /><Cite id="odgers-jensen-2020" /> <Mixed />

- Make the media plan with your child and write it down: which apps, when, where, and what to do when something online upsets them.
- Devices charge outside the bedroom; the last hour before bed is screen-free.
- Keep weekend sleep within an hour or two of weekday sleep.
