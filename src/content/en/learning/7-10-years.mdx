<h2 id="homework">Homework</h2>

Keep homework low-stress. Through grade 6 the link between homework and achievement is weaker than in grades 7-12, at least in the correlational studies that one review of the US research covered, and every one of those studies had design flaws.<Cite id="cooper-2006-homework" />

The kind of involvement matters. Patall et al. (2008) found that when parents were trained to support homework, elementary school children completed more of it, had fewer homework problems and possibly did better at school, and that in correlational studies from elementary to high school, parents setting rules had a stronger link with outcomes than other kinds of involvement.<Cite id="patall-2008-homework" /> Hands-on help is different. Across 448 studies, nearly all kinds of parent involvement, such as talking about school or going to school events, had small positive links with achievement, engagement and motivation, and the exception was homework help, which went with lower achievement, though not with lower motivation.<Cite id="barger-2019" /> Hill and Tyson (2009) found the same exception in 50 middle school studies.<Cite id="hill-tyson-2009" /> <Mixed /> These are <L to="/research/#correlation">correlations</L>, so they cannot tell whether help lowers achievement or whether children who struggle get more help.

- Set the routine together: when, where, and what comes after.
- Be nearby. If your child asks for help, ask what they have tried, then give a hint, not the answer.
- Check that it is done, not that every answer is right. Mistakes show the teacher what to reteach.
- If homework often takes far longer than the school expects or ends in tears, talk to the teacher.

See also <L to="/situations/#homework-now">Homework</L>, and <L to="/toolbox/#routines">Routines and visual schedules</L>.

<h2 id="study">Studying that sticks</h2>

The study habits that feel productive are not always the ones that work. In two experiments, Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students either reread a passage or practice recalling it, and the recall group remembered much more 2 days or 1 week later, even though rereading did better on a test after 5 minutes and made students more confident.<Cite id="roediger-karpicke-2006" /> A later meta-analysis found practice tests more helpful than restudying or any other comparison, though the benefit varied with the learners and the study.<Cite id="adesope-2017" /> Of 50 classroom experiments in one systematic review, most (57%) showed medium or large benefits, though only 6% took place outside WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic) countries.<Cite id="agarwal-2021" /> It works young: first and third graders both benefited from testing in one experiment, but only the third graders realized it.<Cite id="lipowski-2014" />

Spacing is the other half. A major review of 10 study techniques rated practice testing and spaced (distributed) practice as highly useful, because they help learners of different ages and abilities.<Cite id="dunlosky-2013" /> How far apart? Cepeda et al. (2006) pooled 317 experiments and found that the best gap between study sessions grows as the time until the test grows.<Cite id="cepeda-2006" />

- Flashcards: answer on the back, sorted into "know it" and "not yet".
- Close the book, say or write what you remember, then check. Dictation counts.
- For a Friday test, do short sessions on Monday, Wednesday and Thursday rather than one long one on Thursday night.

<h2 id="read">Reading for pleasure</h2>

Once children can read alone, reading can turn into one more piece of homework. Try to keep it a pleasure. Across 99 studies from preschool to university, children and students who read more in their free time tended to read and spell better, and weaker readers seemed to benefit too; the studies show a link, not proof of cause.<Cite id="mol-bus-2011" /> Summer counts: in 41 summer reading programs from kindergarten to grade 8, home programs built around children's own book reading improved reading, and overall the summer programs helped children from low-income families more.<Cite id="kim-quinn-2013" />

- Let your child choose, including comics and long series.
- Keep reading aloud if they still enjoy it.
- Do not pay per book (see <L to="/learning/7-10-years/#motivation">motivation</L>).

<h2 id="numbers">Math at home</h2>

A richer home math environment (math activities, but also attitudes, expectations and talk about math at home) went with higher math achievement across 64 studies (r = .13, see <L to="/research/#effect-sizes">effect sizes</L>); it is a correlation, and the studies differed widely.<Cite id="daucourt-2021" /> Everyday chances: an allowance, shopping, cooking, keeping time, and card games such as the 24 game.

<h2 id="praise">Praise and mindset</h2>

Praise what your child did rather than who they are. Mueller and Dweck (1998) ran six lab studies with fifth graders: children praised for intelligence rather than effort cared more about performing well than about learning, and after a failure they persisted less, enjoyed the task less and did worse.<Cite id="mueller-dweck-1998" /> Among 313 children (average age 10.4), praise for personal qualities, but not for what they did, made children more likely to feel ashamed after failure, especially those with low self-esteem.<Cite id="brummelman-2014" /> <Mixed /> A small long-term study of 53 children found that a higher share of praise for effort and strategies at ages 1-3 predicted better math and reading comprehension in fourth grade, a link that ran through children's belief that intelligence can grow; praise for inborn traits was not linked to later beliefs that ability is fixed.<Cite id="gunderson-2018" /><Cite id="gunderson-2013" /> <Mixed />

<h3 id="growth-mindset">What about growth mindset?</h3>

Your child's school may teach a growth mindset: the belief that ability can grow with effort. In two large meta-analyses, Sisk et al. (2018) and Macnamara and Burgoyne (2023) both found the effects of mindset programs on achievement weak overall, and in the later analysis the small average effect was no longer significant after correcting for possible publication bias, or in the highest-quality studies.<Cite id="sisk-2018" /><Cite id="macnamara-burgoyne-2023" /> <Mixed /> Another meta-analysis found effects (d = 0.14) for targeted groups when programs were delivered as designed, and a national US experiment with teenagers found that a short online program raised lower achievers' grades.<Cite id="burnette-2023" /><Cite id="yeager-2019" /> <Mixed /> Process praise, then, is a sensible daily habit, but do not expect a mindset lesson on its own to transform grades. For everyday praise, see <L to="/toolbox/#specific-praise">Specific praise and attention</L>.

<h2 id="motivation">Motivation</h2>

Children whose parents support their autonomy tend to do better: across 36 studies, that support was linked with higher achievement and with children's own motivation, engagement, positive attitudes toward school and, most strongly, psychological health.<Cite id="vasquez-2016" /> Autonomy support is not permissiveness; researchers treat the two as different things.<Cite id="joussemet-2008" /> In daily life it looks like real choices within your limits, reasons for your rules, and taking your child's point of view seriously. Choice itself helps. In 41 studies, being given choices raised intrinsic motivation, effort and performance, and the boost to motivation was stronger for children than for adults.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" />

Rewards are debated. Deci et al. (1999) pooled 128 studies and found that rewards for doing, finishing or doing well at a task lowered later free-choice interest, with tangible rewards tending to be more harmful for children than for college students.<Cite id="deci-1999" /> A second meta-analysis, by Cameron et al. (2001), disagreed: rewards are generally not harmful, the authors concluded, and rewards tied to the level of performance did not lower motivation.<Cite id="cameron-2001" /> <Mixed /> So whether money for good grades backfires is disputed. Where the two agree is on promised, tangible rewards for tasks that were already interesting, which did harm, and on verbal praise, which helped, though one found praise tended to help children less than college students.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" />

In practice: offer real choices, and do not pay for reading or hobbies your child already enjoys. More in <L to="/toolbox/#choices">Offering choices</L> and <L to="/toolbox/#family-meetings">Family meetings</L>.

<h2 id="focus">Focus, planning and self-control</h2>

Self-control, working memory and mental flexibility are the three core executive functions, and stress, lack of sleep, loneliness and lack of exercise each impair them.<Cite id="diamond-2013" />

These skills can be trained, but there is no quick fix. The programs for children aged 4-12 that improved them, from martial arts and aerobic exercise to games, mindfulness and school curricula, all involved repeated practice that grew harder over time.<Cite id="diamond-lee-2011" /> <Mixed /> Takacs and Kassai (2019) ran a series of meta-analyses on children up to 12 and found no convincing evidence that training gains last; approaches that build these skills indirectly worked as well as or better than drills, were more enjoyable and fit more easily into everyday life.<Cite id="takacs-kassai-2019" /> <Mixed />

- Pick activities that take practice and get harder: a sport, an instrument, chess.
- Help with planning: break big tasks into steps, use a checklist, work in short blocks.

<h2 id="sleep-screens">Sleep and screens</h2>

For children aged 6-12 the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 9-12 hours of sleep in every 24, in a statement the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) endorsed, and that statement links getting enough sleep regularly with better attention, behavior, learning, memory and emotional regulation.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" />

Of 67 studies of school-aged children and teenagers, 90% linked screen time with worse sleep, mainly shorter sleep and later bedtimes, and though cause and effect was not confirmed, the reviewers advise limiting screens, especially before and during bedtime hours.<Cite id="hale-guan-2015" /> The AAP's 2026 policy says most platforms are designed to boost engagement and profit rather than children's health and development, and calls for changes to platform design alongside guidance for families.<Cite id="aap-2026-digital-ecosystems" />

- Make a family screen plan together: when, where, which apps and games.
- Give a warning before time is up, and agree in advance what comes next.
- Charge devices outside the bedroom, and keep the last hour before bed screen-free.
