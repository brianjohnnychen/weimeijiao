title: "Clear expectations and instructions"
summary: "Tell your child exactly what to do, one step at a time and in a calm voice, and keep the house rules the same from day to day."
ages:

agesNote: "From toddlerhood up. Toddlers: one short instruction at a time, said up close and shown with a gesture. Preschoolers: a few simple house rules, repeated often. Once they start school: explain the reasons, and agree on some rules together, for example in [family meetings](/toolbox/#family-meetings)."
evidence: moderate
image: tool-clear-expectations
how:
- "Get your child’s attention first: go to them, get down to eye level and say their name, rather than calling from another room."
- "Say what to do, not what to stop: “Walk, please” instead of “Don’t run.”"
- "Make it a calm statement, not a question: “Shoes on, please” rather than “Can you put your shoes on?” Only ask if no is a fine answer."
- "Give one instruction at a time, in a few words, then wait quietly for a few seconds (count to five in your head)."
- "If your child tends to dig in, start with one or two easy requests they enjoy (“Give me a high five!”), then give the harder one."
- "When your child does it, notice it: “Thank you, shoes on!”"
- "If nothing happens, say it once more, firmly, then follow through the same way every time: help them do it, or use the consequence you planned, such as [when-then](/toolbox/#when-then) or [time-out](/toolbox/#time-out)."
- "Keep house rules few, say them as what to do (“In our family, we speak kindly”), and agree on them with the other adults who care for your child."
mistakes:
- "Asking when you mean telling: “Shall we have a bath?” invites a no."
- "Vague instructions such as “Be good” or “Stop it” that don’t say what to do."
- "Several instructions in one breath: “Put your shoes on, get your bag and find your hat.”"
- "Repeating the same instruction over and over, louder each time, and then letting it drop."
- "Rules that change with your mood, or that differ between the adults at home."
- "Shouting at or shaming your child when they don’t comply."
say:
- "Shoes on, please. Then we can go."
- "Walk inside, please."
- "Toys go back in the box."
- "Gentle hands with the cat."
- "In our family, we speak kindly to each other."

A review of 42 studies of children and teenagers aged 1 to 19 concluded that the way instructions are delivered (“effective instruction delivery”) and a run of easy requests the child is likely to follow before the harder one (“high-probability” request sequences) are among the strategies that may be considered evidence-based for helping children follow adult requests.<Cite id="radley-dart-2016" /> Because those studies ran from toddlers to teenagers, not every finding will apply at every age. Consistency may matter too: in an analysis of 77 parenting program evaluations, programs that taught time-out together with the importance of being consistent had larger effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" />

What you do when your child does not comply also counts. A systematic review of 41 studies of children aged 1.5 to 11 found that verbal reprimands and disapproving nonverbal responses consistently increased compliance, while praise had mixed effects.<Cite id="owen-2012" /> Firm is not the same as harsh, though: in its 2018 policy statement, the American Academy of Pediatrics said that yelling at or shaming children is minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> So when you correct your child, keep it calm and firm, with no shouting or shaming.
