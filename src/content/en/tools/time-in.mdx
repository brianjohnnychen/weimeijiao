Time-in is meant to help a child connect with the parent, put feelings into words and learn to calm down. It has been recommended in positive parenting writing since at least the 1990s, yet the authors of the only study we found write that few studies have tested it.<Cite id="holden-2022" />

In that small pilot study, mothers of 3- to 5-year-olds had brief training and then used time-in for 2 weeks. Of the 32 mothers interviewed at the start, 19 provided mostly complete data, and the mothers rated time-in as easy to use and effective.<Cite id="holden-2022" /> That is encouraging but early: the results are the mothers' own ratings over 2 weeks, and we found no study of whether time-in changes children's behavior over months.

Time-in is sometimes presented as a replacement for time-out. A review that answered attachment-based criticism of time-out concluded that time-out, used in line with current knowledge, generally enhances children's well-being.<Cite id="dadds-tully-2019" /> So the two can sit side by side: time-in for big feelings, a brief time-out for specific behaviors.
