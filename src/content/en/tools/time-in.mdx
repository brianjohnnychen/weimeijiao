The idea behind time-in is simple: stay with your child so they can reconnect with you, put the feeling into words and learn to calm down. Positive parenting books and articles have recommended it since at least the 1990s, yet the authors of the one study we found, Holden et al. (2022), note that few studies have tested it.<Cite id="holden-2022" />

Their study was a small pilot. Mothers of 3- to 5-year-olds had brief training and then used time-in for 2 weeks; of the 32 interviewed at the start, 19 provided mostly complete data, and those mothers rated time-in easy to use and effective.<Cite id="holden-2022" /> Encouraging, but early. The results are the mothers' own ratings over 2 weeks, and nothing we found tracks whether time-in changes a child's behavior over months.

You may have read that time-in should replace time-out. Dadds and Tully (2019), answering attachment-based criticism of time-out, concluded that time-out used in line with current knowledge generally enhances children's well-being.<Cite id="dadds-tully-2019" /> The two can sit side by side: time-in for big feelings, a brief time-out for specific behaviors.
