Bedtime is where the evidence is most direct. Mindell et al. (2009) randomized 405 mothers and their babies or toddlers either to follow a consistent nightly bedtime routine for 2 weeks or to keep their usual one, and the children in the routine group fell asleep faster and woke less often and for shorter periods; the mothers' mood improved too.<Cite id="mindell-2009" /> If bedtime is still a battle, behavioral approaches in general have good support. Mindell et al. (2006) reviewed 52 treatment studies and found them effective for bedtime problems and night wakings in babies and young children.<Cite id="mindell-2006" />

The research on routines in general reaches further back and is looser. Family routines and rituals were related to parenting competence, children's adjustment and marital satisfaction across the 50 years of research Fiese et al. (2002) reviewed, but those studies measured routines in different ways, relied on convenience samples and did not separate direct from indirect effects, so they show links rather than proof that routines cause the benefits.<Cite id="fiese-2002" /> As for the chart itself, we found no studies of picture schedules for typically developing children at home. It is a practical way to make a routine visible, not a tested treatment.
