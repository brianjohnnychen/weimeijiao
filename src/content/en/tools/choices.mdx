how:
- "Decide first what is not up for discussion: we are leaving, teeth get brushed, the seatbelt goes on."
- "Offer two options you are truly fine with: “Red cup or blue cup?” “Hop or tiptoe to the bath?”"
- "Keep them small and concrete. With toddlers, hold up the actual things."
- "Offer the choice early, before a standoff starts."
- "If they suggest another option that is fine with you, go with it. If not, repeat the two choices once."
- "If your child doesn’t choose within a few seconds, choose for them, calmly: “I’ll choose this time. Blue it is.”"
- "As your child gets older, hand over bigger choices with real responsibility: the order of tasks, when homework happens, how to spend pocket money."
mistakes:
- "Offering a choice you won’t accept: “Do you want to go to bed now?”"
- "Too many options or an open question, such as “What do you want for breakfast?” on a rushed morning."
- "A threat dressed up as a choice: “Either you stop it or you’ll be sorry.”"
- "Giving in when your child refuses both options and starts to bargain."
- "Offering choices about safety. The car seat is not optional; which song plays on the way can be."

Direct research on choices as a discipline tool is scarce, so the case rests on related studies of motivation. Patall et al. (2008) pooled 41 studies with children and adults and found that being given a choice increased intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance and feelings of competence; the boost to motivation was stronger for children than for adults, stronger when no reward followed the choice, and stronger for small choices that left the task itself unchanged than for choices between activities.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" />

A choice is also a small act of autonomy support. Joussemet et al. (2008) draw a line between parents’ autonomy support and permissiveness, and their review finds that support consistently linked with positive outcomes across different types of studies and at different ages; in observation studies, it went with toddlers taking rules on as their own (what researchers call internalization).<Cite id="joussemet-2008" /> None of this work watched everyday cooperation at home. It measured motivation, tasks or associations, so choices count here as a sensible, low-cost tool rather than a proven one.
