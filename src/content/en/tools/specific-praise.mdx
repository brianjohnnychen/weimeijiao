how:
- "Catch your child being good: watch for moments of the behavior you want more of, such as playing calmly, sharing or getting dressed."
- "Respond soon, while it is happening or right after."
- "Use your face, voice and touch as well as words: a smile, a thumbs up or a hand on the shoulder all count."
- "Say what you liked: “You put your plate in the sink. Thank you.” Naming the behavior shows what you noticed, though a simple “Well done!” is fine too."
- "Praise what your child did, such as effort, kindness or a good choice, rather than who they are."
- "Aim for far more warm moments than corrections over the day."
- "Combine it with the other tools: praise the behavior you asked for, and use [planned ignoring](/toolbox/#planned-ignoring) for minor behavior you want to see less of."

- "Saving your attention for misbehavior and saying nothing when things go well."
- "Praise with a sting in the tail: “Good job cleaning up. Why can’t you do that every day?”"

- "Automatic or over-the-top praise that doesn’t match what actually happened."

Parenting programs teach a long list of techniques, and few of them stand out on their own. When Leijten et al. (2019) pooled randomized program trials in two meta-analyses, only 3 of the 26 techniques were linked with stronger effects on disruptive behavior: positive reinforcement, praise in particular, and natural or logical consequences.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

Still, praise is one part of the whole approach, not a switch on its own. Praise did not increase children’s compliance with instructions in experiments that each tested a single parenting behavior,<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> and across the 41 studies in one systematic review, its effects on compliance were mixed.<Cite id="owen-2012" /> Being specific may matter less than people say: in the two experiments Leijten et al. (2016) ran with children aged 3 to 9, praise that named the behavior worked no better than general praise.<Cite id="leijten-2016" />

What you praise may matter too. Kamins and Dweck (1999) ran a small role-play experiment with 5- and 6-year-olds in which children praised as a person showed more helpless reactions after a later setback, including blaming themselves, than children praised for their effort or approach.<Cite id="kamins-dweck-1999" /> Among 313 children around age 10, Brummelman et al. (2014) found that person praise made children, especially those with low self-esteem, more likely to feel ashamed after failure.<Cite id="brummelman-2014" /> These were short experiments. Treat this as a sensible lean, not a firm rule.
