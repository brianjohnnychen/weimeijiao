how:
- "Catch your child being good: watch for moments of the behavior you want more of, such as playing calmly, sharing or getting dressed."
- "Respond soon, while it is happening or right after."
- "Use your face, voice and touch as well as words: a smile, a thumbs up or a hand on the shoulder all count."
- "Say what you liked: “You put your plate in the sink. Thank you.” Naming the behavior shows what you noticed, though a simple “Well done!” is fine too."
- "Praise what your child did, such as effort, kindness or a good choice, rather than who they are."
- "Aim for far more warm moments than corrections over the day."
- "Combine it with the other tools: praise the behavior you asked for, and use [planned ignoring](/toolbox/#planned-ignoring) for minor behavior you want to see less of."
mistakes:
- "Saving your attention for misbehavior and saying nothing when things go well."
- "Praise with a sting in the tail: “Good job tidying up. Why can’t you do that every day?”"
- "Praising your child as a person (“good girl”, “so smart”) rather than what they did."
- "Automatic or over-the-top praise that doesn’t match what actually happened."
- "Expecting praise alone to get instructions followed. In experiments, praise on its own did not raise compliance.[[cite:compliance-plos-2018]]"

Praise is one of the few parenting techniques linked with stronger program effects. Two meta-analyses of randomized parenting program trials found that only 3 of 26 techniques were linked with stronger effects on disruptive behavior: positive reinforcement, praise in particular, and natural or logical consequences.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

But praise is one part of the whole approach, not a switch on its own. In experiments that each tested a single parenting behavior, praise did not increase children’s compliance with instructions,<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> and a systematic review of 41 studies found mixed effects of praise on compliance.<Cite id="owen-2012" /> Being specific may matter less than often claimed: in two experiments with children aged 3 to 9, praise that named the behavior worked no better than general praise.<Cite id="leijten-2016" />

What you praise may also matter. In a small role-play experiment with 5- and 6-year-olds, children praised as a person showed more helpless reactions after a later setback, including blaming themselves, than children praised for their effort or approach.<Cite id="kamins-dweck-1999" /> In a study of 313 children around age 10, person praise made children, especially those with low self-esteem, more likely to feel ashamed after failure.<Cite id="brummelman-2014" /> These were short experiments, so treat this as a sensible lean, not a firm rule.
