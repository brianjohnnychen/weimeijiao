title: "Time-out, used correctly"
summary: "A short, calm break from attention for a behavior you named in advance, given after one clear warning and followed by a fresh start."
ages:
- 3-5-years
- 5-7-years
agesNote: "Most of the studies here involved preschoolers, and the large analysis of parenting program components covered children aged 0 to 7.[[cite:kaminski-2008]] For toddlers, start with [redirection](/toolbox/#redirection) and [time-in](/toolbox/#time-in). For school-age children, a short [privilege removal](/toolbox/#privilege-removal) is another option."
evidence: strong
image: tool-time-out
how:
- "Pick one or two behaviors that lead to time-out, such as hitting, or not following an instruction after a warning. Explain them to your child at a calm time."
- "Choose a boring, safe spot where you can see your child, such as a chair or a step away from toys and screens. Never lock a door."
- "Give one clear warning. If the behavior happens again, say in one sentence what happened and that it's time-out, then stop talking."
- "Keep it short. A few minutes is enough: set a timer and end it when the time is up, even if your child is still grumbling. See [short time-outs](/little-time/#short-time-out)."
- "During time-out, don't talk, argue or bargain. Stay calm and nearby."
- "If your child gets up early, calmly bring them back and start the time again, or use the back-up you planned, such as losing a privilege later. Never hit as a back-up. More help: [if your child refuses time-out](/approach/#refuses-time-out)."
- "When it ends, start fresh, with no lecture. If the time-out was for not doing something, ask again and praise your child when they do it."
- "Keep the rest of the day warm, with plenty of attention, play and [specific praise](/toolbox/#specific-praise). Time-out is a short break from that attention."
mistakes:
- "Making it long. We found no study showing that longer time-outs work better, and short ones are easier to carry out."
- "Giving time-out in anger, with yelling, shaming or threats. Calm and boring is the point."
- "Surprise time-outs for behaviors your child didn't know were off-limits."
- "Ending it early because your child pleads or protests. Keep the short time you set."
- "Using a scary, dark or locked place. The spot should be boring and safe, and you should be able to see your child."
- "Using it for everything. Save it for the behaviors you named, and use [planned ignoring](/toolbox/#planned-ignoring) for minor fussing."
say:
- "Hitting hurts. If you hit again, you'll go to time-out."
- "You hit, so it's time-out. Sit here until the timer rings."
- "Time-out is over. Let's try again: please put the blocks in the box."
- "You put them all away. Thank you, that was quick!"

Time-out is one of the best-studied tools here. Across 77 evaluations of parenting programs for children aged 0 to 7, programs that taught time-out and consistency had larger effects,<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> and a meta-analysis of 19 experimental studies found that time-out for not following instructions robustly increased children's compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> In a US study that followed families from early childhood to fifth grade, children whose parents reported using time-out at age 3 did not differ from other children on any emotional, behavioral or parent-child relationship outcome.<Cite id="knight-2020" /> Responding to the criticism, one review concluded that careful use within parenting programs is justified and helps many children with conduct problems.<Cite id="morawska-sanders-2011" /> Another, answering the worry that time-out harms attachment, concluded that time-out used in line with current knowledge generally enhances children's well-being.<Cite id="dadds-tully-2019" />

The practical details rest on much smaller studies. In 2 tiny studies, with 4 and 3 children, ending time-out after a fixed time worked as well as waiting until the child had stopped misbehaving, and in the first, misbehaving during time-out did not predict behavior outside it.<Cite id="donaldson-vollmer-2011" /><Cite id="mace-1986" /> In a study of 6 preschool boys, making time-out shorter whenever a boy went straight away helped 4 of them go to time-out when asked.<Cite id="donaldson-2013" /> In a clinic study of 16 preschoolers, a non-physical back-up for leaving time-out worked as well as spanking, and the authors concluded that enforcing a minimum time-out length was critical in that setting.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" />

What is not known: the best length. We found no study showing that longer time-outs work better, and a review of 30 years of research found that the way time-out was carried out varied widely and often was not reported.<Cite id="everett-2010" /> One cross-sectional study of mothers and their 8- to 12-year-olds in 6 countries, including China, linked time-out with more anxiety in children, as it did corporal punishment, shaming and expressing disappointment. A study like that cannot show which came first.<Cite id="gershoff-2010-international" />
