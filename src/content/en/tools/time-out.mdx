For a school-age child, a short [privilege removal](/toolbox/#privilege-removal) is another option."

how:
- "Pick one or two behaviors that lead to time-out, such as hitting, or not following an instruction after a warning. Explain them to your child at a calm time."
- "Choose a boring, safe spot where you can see your child, such as a chair or a step away from toys and screens. Never lock a door."
- "Give one clear warning. If the behavior happens again, say in one sentence what happened and that it's time-out, then stop talking."
- "Keep it short, just a few minutes. Set a timer and end it when the time is up, even if your child is still grumbling. See [short time-outs](/little-time/#short-time-out)."
- "During time-out, don't talk, argue or bargain. Stay calm and nearby."
- "If your child gets up early, calmly bring them back and start the time again, or use the back-up you planned, such as losing a privilege later. Never hit as a back-up. More help: [if your child refuses time-out](/approach/#refuses-time-out)."
- "When it ends, start fresh, with no lecture. If the time-out was for not doing something, ask again and praise your child when they do it."
- "Keep the rest of the day warm, with plenty of attention, play and [specific praise](/toolbox/#specific-praise). Time-out is a short break from that attention."
mistakes:
- "Making it long. We found no study showing that longer time-outs work better for young children at home, and short ones are easier to carry out."
- "Giving time-out in anger, with yelling, shaming or threats. Calm and boring is the point."
- "Surprise time-outs for behaviors your child didn't know were off-limits."
- "Ending it early because your child pleads or protests. Keep the short time you set."
- "Using a scary, dark or locked place. The spot should be boring and safe, and you should be able to see your child."
- "Using it for everything. Save it for the behaviors you named, and use [planned ignoring](/toolbox/#planned-ignoring) for minor fussing."
say:
- "Hitting hurts. If you hit again, you'll go to time-out."
- "You hit, so it's time-out. Sit here until the timer rings."
- "Time-out is over. Let's try again: please put the blocks in the box."
- "You put them all away. Thank you, that was quick!"

Few discipline methods have been used as widely, or studied as much, as time-out.<Cite id="dadds-tully-2019" /> Programs that taught it, together with consistency, had larger effects across the 77 evaluations of parenting programs for children aged 0 to 7 that Kaminski et al. (2008) analyzed,<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> and across 19 experimental studies pooled in one meta-analysis, time-out for not following instructions robustly increased children's compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> Does it leave a mark later? Knight et al. (2020) followed US families from early childhood to fifth grade, and children whose parents reported using time-out at age 3 did not differ significantly from other children on any emotional, behavioral or parent-child relationship outcome.<Cite id="knight-2020" /> Two reviews have weighed the criticisms. One concluded that careful use within parenting programs is justified and helps many children with conduct problems,<Cite id="morawska-sanders-2011" /> and Dadds and Tully (2019), answering the worry that time-out harms attachment, concluded that time-out used in line with current knowledge generally enhances children's well-being.<Cite id="dadds-tully-2019" />

The practical details rest on much smaller studies. Two tiny ones agree on one point: Donaldson and Vollmer (2011) worked with 4 children aged 3 and 4, a second study with 3 children, 2 of them with intellectual disabilities, and in both time-out reduced problem behavior whether it ended after a fixed time or lasted until the child had stopped misbehaving.<Cite id="donaldson-vollmer-2011" /><Cite id="mace-1986" /> In the first of those, misbehaving during time-out did not predict misbehavior outside it.<Cite id="donaldson-vollmer-2011" /> Among 6 preschool boys, making time-out shorter whenever a boy went straight away helped 4 of them go to time-out when asked.<Cite id="donaldson-2013" /> And in a clinic study of 16 noncompliant preschoolers, Day and Roberts (1983) found that a back-up for leaving time-out that did not involve spanking worked as well as spanking; they concluded that enforcing a minimum time-out length was critical in that setting.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" />

The best length is not known. We found no study showing that longer time-outs work better for young children at home, and when Everett et al. (2010) reviewed 30 years of research, the way time-out was carried out varied widely and often was not reported.<Cite id="everett-2010" /> Not every finding is reassuring. Gershoff et al. (2010) surveyed mothers and their 8- to 12-year-olds in 6 countries, including China, and in that cross-sectional study time-out was linked with more anxiety symptoms in children, as were corporal punishment, shaming and expressing disappointment; a study like that cannot show which came first.<Cite id="gershoff-2010-international" />
