With school-age children, plan together what each of you will do differently next time. Preteens: a plain apology with no lecture attached; they notice when you repair, and copy it."
evidence: emerging
image: tool-repair
how:
- "Calm down first. If you need a minute, say so and step away, as long as your child is safe."
- "Come back soon, the same day if you can, and get down to your child's eye level."
- "Own your part in a sentence or two, without excuses and without blaming your child."
- "Reconnect in whatever way your child welcomes: a hug, sitting together, or a few minutes of play."
- "If a rule was broken, it still stands. Repair mends the relationship; it doesn't cancel the limit."
- "Make a plan for next time, for you and for your child."
- "When your child hurts someone, help them repair too: check on the other person or help fix what broke. Invite a sorry when they are ready rather than forcing one."
- "If you are losing your temper often, look for support. See [what to do in the moment instead](/physical-discipline/#instead) and [repair after losing your temper](/physical-discipline/#repair)."
mistakes:
- "Waiting for your child to apologize first. You are the adult, so you go first."
- "Adding a but that hands the blame back, such as saying your child made you yell."
- "Dropping the limit to make up. You can repair and still keep the rule."
- "A long, emotional apology that leaves your child comforting you. Keep it short and about them."
- "Treating repair as a fix for frequent yelling. The goal is fewer blowups; repair is for the ones that still happen."
say:
- "I yelled at you. That wasn't OK, and I'm sorry."
- "I was upset about the mess, but yelling wasn't the right way to tell you."
- "Can I have a hug? I love you, even when we're both upset."
- "Next time I feel that angry, I'm going to take a break first. What could help you next time?"
- "The toys still need to go away. Let's do it together."

We found no trials testing whether a parent's apology or repair after conflict changes young children's behavior, so this rating rests on related research. A review of research on babies describes infants and caregivers using emotional expressions to regulate their interactions with each other. It suggests that healthy development goes with interactions in which mismatches are frequently repaired, and poorer development with long stretches of failed interaction and negative emotion.<Cite id="tronick-1989" /> That work is about everyday moments with babies, not about apologizing after yelling.

Repair is also no substitute for avoiding harsh words. In its 2018 policy statement, the American Academy of Pediatrics advised against verbal abuse such as threatening, insulting or shaming children, and said that yelling at or shaming children is minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> In a longitudinal study of 976 families, harsh verbal discipline at age 13 was linked with increases in conduct problems and depressive symptoms over the next year, and parents' warmth did not weaken that link. The link also ran the other way: misbehavior at 13 was linked with increases in parents' harsh verbal discipline over the next year.<Cite id="wang-kenny-2014" /> That study involved teenagers, not young children, and it did not test repair.
