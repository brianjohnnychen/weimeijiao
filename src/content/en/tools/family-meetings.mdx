how:
- "Pick a regular time, such as Sunday after dinner, and keep it short: 10-20 minutes."
- "Start with something good. Each person says one thing someone else did well this week."
- "Take one problem at a time, and let anyone raise a topic, including your child."
- "Work through it the way you would when [solving problems together](/toolbox/#problem-solving): everyone says what is hard for them, you list ideas, and you pick one to try."
- "Give children a real say, but keep the final word on safety, health and money. Giving children a voice is part of what researchers call autonomy support, which is not the same as being permissive.[[cite:joussemet-2008]]"
- "Write down what you agreed, put it on the fridge, and check on it at the next meeting. Rules you agree on can go on our [family rules poster](/printables/family-rules/)."
- "End with something fun: plan an outing, pick a game, or share a snack."

- "Saving discipline for the meeting. Meetings are for planning; a fight happening right now needs handling right now."

We found no studies that tested family meetings directly, so this rating rests on related research.

Meetings borrow two ideas that have been studied. One is solving problems together: in a randomized trial with children aged 7 to 14 who had oppositional defiant disorder, a collaborative problem-solving approach worked as well as standard parent management training.<Cite id="ollendick-2016" /> The other is giving children a real voice: a meta-analysis of 36 studies linked parents' support for children's autonomy with better psychological health and school achievement.<Cite id="vasquez-2016" /> The autonomy studies show links rather than cause and effect, and none of this research looked at family meetings. Whether regular meetings improve behavior or family relationships is not known, so treat them as a practical way to plan together, not a proven treatment.
