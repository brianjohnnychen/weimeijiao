how:
- "Pick a calm time, not the middle of a fight. Use it for problems that keep coming back, such as mornings, screen time, homework or sharing a room."
- "Start with your child's side. Ask what is hard for them and listen without arguing. Then say your own concern in one sentence."
- "Name the problem in a way that includes both concerns, for example: you want more time to play, and I need homework done before dinner."
- "List ideas together without judging them yet. Let your child go first."
- "Pick one idea you can both live with. You keep the final say on safety and health."
- "Agree on when you will check how it is going, and adjust the plan together if it isn't working."
- "For fights between siblings, be the mediator rather than the judge: help each child say what they want and how they feel, then let them suggest solutions. When Siddiqui and Ross (2004) trained mothers to do this, the mothers could use it, and their children reasoned and talked about feelings more often than children in control families.[[cite:siddiqui-ross-2004]]"
mistakes:
- "Trying to solve it in the middle of a meltdown. Wait until everyone is calm."
- "Coming in with the answer already decided. If your child's ideas never count, it isn't solving problems together."
- "Skipping your child's concern and jumping straight to yours."
- "Agreeing to a plan you won't keep. Only accept ideas you can live with."
- "Dropping the plan after one bad day. Check in and adjust it together."
say:
- "I've noticed mornings have been hard. What's tough for you about getting ready?"
- "So you want more time to play, and I need us out the door by 7:30. What could we try?"
- "Let's write down every idea, even the silly ones."
- "Which idea should we try this week? Let's check on Friday how it went."
- "You both want the swing. What could you do so you each get a turn?"

Sitting down to solve a problem together can sound soft. In clinic trials it has held up well with children whose behavior problems were serious. Collaborative & Proactive Solutions, a collaborative problem-solving approach, worked as well as standard parent management training in a randomized trial of 134 children aged 7 to 14 with oppositional defiant disorder: about half the children in each treatment no longer met the diagnosis and were much improved, none on the waitlist did, and when Ollendick et al. (2015) checked again 6 months later the gains had held.<Cite id="ollendick-2016" /> Greene et al. (2004) had earlier tried the approach with 47 children with oppositional defiant disorder who had trouble regulating their emotions, and the improvements matched, and often exceeded, those from parent training.<Cite id="greene-2004" /> Problem-solving skills training also helped in a randomized trial Kazdin et al. (1992) ran with 97 children aged 7 to 13 with severe antisocial behavior, and combining it with parent training helped more.<Cite id="kazdin-1992-psst" />

Everyday squabbles have a thinner record. When Siddiqui and Ross (2004) trained mothers of 5- to 8-year-olds to mediate their children's disputes, the mothers could do it, mothers and children both liked it, and the children reasoned, talked about feelings and understood each other's motives more often than children in control families; the long-term effects are not known.<Cite id="siddiqui-ross-2004" /> Most of this research ran through therapists and involved children with serious behavior problems. Ordinary family conflicts, and children under 5, have been studied much less. One caution: across the 77 evaluations of parenting programs for children aged 0 to 7 that Kaminski et al. (2008) analyzed, the programs that taught parents problem solving were linked with smaller effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" />
