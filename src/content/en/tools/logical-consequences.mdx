---
title: "Logical consequences"
summary: "A small, short consequence you set up that connects to what your child did, such as a thrown ball going on the shelf until after lunch."
ages:
- 1-3-years
- 3-5-years
- 5-7-years
- 7-10-years
agesNote: "Toddlers need the simplest form: the consequence happens right away and is over soon, like a thrown toy going up on the shelf for a few minutes. From preschool age on, you can tell your child the consequence ahead of time and talk about it afterwards. School-age children can help you decide what is fair."
evidence: moderate
image: tool-logical-consequences
how:
- "Decide ahead of time which consequence goes with which behavior, and tell your child before it happens. Unlike a [natural consequence](/toolbox/#natural-consequences), this one is set up by you."
- "Make it connect to the behavior. Misuse a thing, lose it for a while. Make a mess, help clean it up. Go past the screen limit, lose the rest of today's screen time."
- "Keep it small and short, so your child gets another try soon. Choose something you will be able to carry out every time."
- "Give one calm reminder. If it happens again, follow through in one short sentence, without a lecture."
- "When the time is up, give the thing back or let your child try again, and notice out loud when they get it right."
- "This is one of the brief, consistent consequences in the [best-proven approach](/approach/#consequences). Use it alongside plenty of praise and attention for what goes well."
mistakes:
- "Making it big or long, like a week without toys. It is hard to keep up, and your child has no fresh chance to do better."
- "Inventing a consequence in anger. Plan it while you are calm."
- "Picking something unrelated, such as no dessert for not cleaning up. The link to the behavior is what makes it logical."
- "Lecturing, shaming or adding threats while you follow through. One calm sentence is enough."
- "Announcing a consequence and then not following through."
- "Using consequences for skills your child is still learning. Teach and practice those instead."
say:
- "If the ball gets thrown in the house again, it goes on the shelf until after lunch."
- "The ball is going on the shelf now. You can try again after lunch."
- "The crayon went on the wall. Let's get a cloth and clean it off together."
- "Screen time went past the timer, so screens are done for today. Tomorrow is a new try."

Natural and logical consequences are among the few parts of parenting programs linked with stronger effects. In 2 meta-analyses of randomized trials, researchers looked at 26 techniques that parenting programs teach. Only 3 were linked with stronger effects on disruptive behavior: positive reinforcement, praise in particular, and natural or logical consequences.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

That finding is about programs that include the technique, not a test of logical consequences on their own, and no technique in that analysis was linked with stronger long-term effects.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> We found no studies comparing different logical consequences or testing how long they should last. The advice to keep them small, related and short is practical guidance, not a tested rule.
