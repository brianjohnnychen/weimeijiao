agesNote: "From around age 2, with short, immediate pairs: “When your hands are washed, then snack.” From preschool on: it fits daily routines, screens and homework. Older children can help plan their own when-then order. Preteens: the pairs get bigger (homework done, then the group chat; room tidy by Saturday, then the sleepover) and they can set the order themselves."
evidence: emerging
image: tool-when-then
how:
- "Put the task first and the good thing second: “When the toys are in the box, then we’ll read a story.”"
- "Choose a “then” that is going to happen anyway, such as going out, a story, playtime or screen time, rather than candy or new toys."
- "Keep the task short and clear, and the “then” soon after it. For young children, think minutes, not hours."
- "Say it once, calmly, as a plain fact rather than a threat or a deal."
- "Follow through both ways: when the task is done, deliver the “then” right away. If it isn’t done, the “then” waits, without a lecture."
- "For young children, you can make a simple first-then card with two pictures."
mistakes:
- "Turning it into a threat: “If you don’t clean up, no park!”"
- "Handing over the “then” first: “Fine, watch your show, but clean up afterwards.”"
- "Adding new conditions after your child has done the task."
- "Pairing it with a task your child can’t do yet, or one that takes far too long for their age."
- "Using candy or toys as the reward for things your child already enjoys doing. In experiments, tangible rewards promised in advance lowered children’s later interest in activities they already liked.[[cite:lepper-1973]][[cite:deci-1999]]"
say:
- "When your shoes are on, then we’ll go to the park."
- "First pajamas, then story."
- "When your homework is done, the tablet is yours for half an hour."
- "As soon as the toys are away, we can go outside."

There is little direct research on when-then itself. It uses the same principle as positive reinforcement: the wanted behavior is followed by something your child values. Two meta-analyses of randomized parenting program trials found that positive reinforcement was one of only 3 of 26 techniques linked with stronger effects on disruptive behavior.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

One caution concerns rewards. Researchers disagree about how much rewards affect motivation, but analyses on both sides found that tangible rewards promised in advance can lower interest in a task a child already finds interesting.<Cite id="deci-1999" /><Cite id="cameron-2001" /> So save when-then for tasks your child would not choose to do, and use everyday activities as the “then.”
