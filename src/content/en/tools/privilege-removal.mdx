how:
- "Decide ahead of time which privilege goes with which behavior, and tell your child before it happens."
- "Choose something small and related to the problem: the game the children fought over, the scooter that went into the road, the screen time that ran past the timer."
- "Give one warning. If it happens again, remove the privilege calmly, in one sentence."
- "Keep it short: the rest of the afternoon or evening, not a week, so your child gets a fresh chance soon. See [quick privilege removal](/little-time/#privilege-removal)."
- "Don't add more when your child argues. Say it once and move on."
- "When the time is up, give it back without a lecture, and notice when your child uses it well."
- "Never take away meals, sleep, comfort objects or time with you."
mistakes:
- "Removing something big or for a long time, like a week without screens. It is hard to keep up, and your child has no fresh chance to do better."
- "Stacking removals during an argument: no tablet, no park, no dessert. Pick one and stop."
- "Taking away something unrelated, or something your child needs, such as food, sleep or a comfort object."
- "Announcing a removal in anger and dropping it later. Only say what you will carry out."
- "Making it your main tool. Keep praise and attention for good behavior far more frequent than removals."

Privilege removal is one of the brief, calm consequences in the <L to="/approach/#consequences">best-proven approach</L>, but direct evidence for it on its own is thin. In a small classroom study of 4 aggressive preschoolers, teachers took away a smiley face, with a reprimand, after each aggressive act, and children who kept at least one face chose a reward. Aggression dropped substantially, and teachers and parents found the system highly acceptable.<Cite id="reynolds-kelley-1997" /> A removal that is related to the behavior is a kind of logical consequence, and natural and logical consequences were among the few techniques linked with stronger results in parenting programs.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

What is not known: we found no study of parents removing privileges at home with young children, and none comparing short removals with long ones. The advice to keep removals small, related, announced and brief is practical guidance, not a tested rule.
