Direct evidence for privilege removal on its own is thin, even though it is one of the brief, calm consequences in the <L to="/approach/#consequences">best-proven approach</L>. The one direct study is small: in a classroom with 4 aggressive preschoolers, teachers took away a smiley face, with a reprimand, after each aggressive act, and a child who kept at least one face chose a reward. Reynolds and Kelley (1997) found that aggression dropped substantially and that teachers and parents rated the system highly acceptable.<Cite id="reynolds-kelley-1997" /> A removal tied to the behavior is a kind of logical consequence, and natural and logical consequences were among the few techniques linked with stronger results in parenting programs.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

Nobody has studied parents removing privileges at home with young children, as far as we could find, and nobody has compared short removals with long ones. So the card's advice (small, related, announced in advance, brief) rests on practice rather than on a study.
