summary: "Steer your child away from what they shouldn’t be doing and toward something they can do, ideally before trouble builds."

agesNote: "Mainly for babies and toddlers. Preschoolers can take a short reason or a choice along with the swap. Later it turns into suggesting a better way to do what they want: the ball goes outside instead of around the living room."
evidence: emerging
image: tool-redirection
how:
- "Move in early, at the first sign of trouble: a hand reaching for the remote, a tug-of-war starting over a toy."
- "With a baby, simply pick them up or move the object, and offer something else interesting."
- "From toddlerhood, name the limit in a few calm words: “Not the cord. Here’s your ball.”"
- "Offer something that meets the same need: something to bang, throw, climb or squeeze."
- "Make the new option appealing: get down and join in for a minute."
- "If that doesn’t work, change the scene: another room, a look out of the window, a song."
- "Head off repeat battles by moving fragile or dangerous things out of reach."
mistakes:
- "Distracting without ever naming the limit. From toddlerhood, say the rule in a few words too."
- "Reaching for a screen or a snack as the distraction every time."
- "Trying to distract when there is real danger. Stop your child first; see [keeping your child safe](/physical-discipline/#safety-vs-punishment)."
- "Redirecting a child who is hurt or upset instead of comforting them first. See [time-in](/toolbox/#time-in)."
- "Offering a swap that doesn’t meet the need, such as a puzzle for a child who wants to climb."
say:
- "Books are for reading. Let’s find your drum."
- "Gentle hands. You can squeeze this pillow."
- "Balls are for outside. Indoors we can roll this soft one."
- "The table isn’t for climbing. Want to climb the cushion mountain?"
- "Ooh, look what’s outside the window!"

A baby reaching for the power cord gets moved, and handed something else; parents do this long before anyone shows them a technique. Redirection itself has barely been studied, so its case rests on expert guidance and on what toddlers are like. The American Academy of Pediatrics, in its 2018 policy statement advising against physical punishment and verbal abuse such as shaming, listed redirecting among the alternatives it recommended, alongside positive reinforcement of appropriate behavior, setting limits and setting expectations for the future.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" />

What toddlers are like matters here, because hitting starts early. Alink et al. (2006) found physical aggression already present at 12 months in a large sample of young children; it rose during the second year of life and declined from the third birthday onward.<Cite id="alink-2006" /> Your job at this age is to keep everyone safe and steer your child toward something better. How much of that later decline comes from redirection, and how much from simply growing older, is not known.
