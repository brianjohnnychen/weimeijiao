<h2 id="normal">What's normal at this age</h2>

<h3 id="tantrums">Tantrums</h3>

Tantrums are still part of the picture. In a community study of 1,490 preschoolers, 83.7% had tantrums sometimes, but only 8.6% had them every day.<Cite id="wakschlag-2012" /> Ordinary tantrums tend to follow frustration in predictable situations; the worrying kind are unpredictable, very long or destructive.<Cite id="wakschlag-2012" />

In a study of 3- to 6-year-olds, violence during tantrums, more tantrums at preschool or daycare and a harder time recovering were more common in children with disruptive behavior disorders than in healthy children, and self-harm during tantrums was more common in children with depression.<Cite id="belden-2008" /> These are differences between groups, not a checklist for your child, but if you see them, talk to your pediatrician (see <L to="/situations/#tantrums-help">tantrums: when to get help</L>).

<h3 id="aggression">Hitting and aggression</h3>

Physical aggression usually starts to decline after the third birthday.<Cite id="alink-2006" /> In a study of more than 10,000 Canadian children aged 2 to 11, most used it less and less until they rarely or almost never did by the preteen years, but about 1 in 6 (16.6%) stayed at a high level.<Cite id="cote-2006" /> If your child's hitting is frequent and not easing, raise it with a professional (see <L to="/approach/#aggression">aggression and hitting</L>).

<h3 id="first-lies">First lies</h3>

When told not to peek at a toy, most 3- to 8-year-olds in one study peeked and then denied it.<Cite id="talwar-lee-2008" /> Among 2- and 3-year-olds, those with stronger executive function, the mental skills behind self-control, were more likely to lie.<Cite id="evans-lee-2013" /> A first lie is a reason to teach, not to panic (see <L to="/situations/#lying-why">lying</L>).

<h3 id="sleep">Sleep</h3>

For 3- to 5-year-olds, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 10-13 hours of sleep in 24 hours, naps included, and regularly getting the recommended amount is linked with better attention, behavior and emotional control.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" />

<h2 id="works">What works, and why</h2>

Every part of <L to="/approach/#core">the core approach</L> applies now. In a meta-analysis of 36 controlled trials with young children (average age 4.7), behavioral treatments had the largest effects on disruptive behavior.<Cite id="comer-2013" /> Programs that built positive parent-child interaction, taught time-out and consistency, and had parents practice with their own child had larger effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> The approach has been tested with Chinese families too: in Hong Kong, parents of 3- to 7-year-olds with early conduct problems who took Triple P reported fewer behavior problems than parents on a waiting list.<Cite id="leung-2003-hongkong" />

<h3 id="clear-rules">Clear rules and instructions</h3>

Choose 3 to 5 house rules, said as what to do: "We use gentle hands." "We walk inside." Put them on the wall with pictures, or use the <L to="/printables/family-rules/">family rules poster</L>.

For instructions, get close, get your child's attention, say one thing, then wait a few seconds: "Please put your cup in the sink," not "Could you stop making a mess?" A review of 42 studies concluded that giving instructions well ("effective instruction delivery") may be considered an evidence-based way to get children to cooperate.<Cite id="radley-dart-2016" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#clear-expectations-how">clear expectations and instructions</L> and, for a child who digs in, <L to="/situations/#defiance-now">defiance and "no"</L>.

<h3 id="praise">Praise the behavior you want</h3>

Catch your child being good, often. In parenting program trials, praise was one of only three of 26 techniques linked with stronger effects on disruptive behavior.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> Naming the behavior ("You put your shoes on by yourself!") shows what you liked, but the exact wording matters less than you might think: in two experiments with 3- to 9-year-olds, labeled praise worked no better than a simple "Well done".<Cite id="leijten-2016" />

Think of praise as building good behavior over time, not as a way to get obedience in the moment. In experiments that tested single parenting behaviors, praise on its own did not increase compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> That is the job of clear instructions and follow-through.

<h3 id="planned-ignoring">Planned ignoring</h3>

For minor, attention-seeking behavior such as whining or silly noises, look away, stay neutral, and give your attention back the moment it stops. In experiments that tested single parenting behaviors, briefly ignoring a child after they did not do what was asked robustly increased compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> Never ignore hitting, danger or destruction. See <L to="/toolbox/#planned-ignoring-how">planned ignoring</L> and <L to="/situations/#whining-now">whining</L>.

<h3 id="time-in-time-out">Time-in and time-out</h3>

They do different jobs. **Time-in** means staying close when feelings take over, naming the feeling and breathing together until the storm passes. It is meant to help a child connect with you, share feelings and learn to calm down, but the evidence is early: in one small pilot study, mothers of 3- to 5-year-olds rated it easy to use and effective, and the authors note how few studies exist.<Cite id="holden-2022" />

**Time-out** is a short, calm break from attention and fun after a clear rule-break, such as hitting or refusing an instruction. In experiments that tested single parenting behaviors, time-out for not following instructions robustly increased compliance.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> Keep it short and boring: one warning, a dull spot, no talking, then back to normal without a lecture, and the original instruction still stands. Crying during time-out does not mean it is failing: in a small study of four 3- and 4-year-olds, problem behavior during time-out did not predict problem behavior outside it.<Cite id="donaldson-vollmer-2011" /> Leaving time-out can be handled without hitting: in a small clinic study of 16 preschoolers, a non-physical backup worked as well as spanking.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" />

Worried that time-out harms children? In a long-term study, children whose parents used time-out at age 3 did not differ from other children on any emotional, behavioral or relationship outcome through fifth grade.<Cite id="knight-2020" /> A review that answers attachment-based criticism argues that time-out used well generally supports children's well-being.<Cite id="dadds-tully-2019" />

A rough rule: time-in when your child is overwhelmed, time-out for a rule-break you want to see less often. See the <L to="/toolbox/#time-in-how">time-in</L> and <L to="/toolbox/#time-out-how">time-out</L> cards, <L to="/little-time/#short-time-out">short time-outs</L> and, for a child who won't stay put, <L to="/approach/#refuses-time-out">refusing time-out</L>.

<h3 id="consequences">Logical consequences</h3>

A logical consequence fits the problem, is stated calmly, stays short and ends with a fresh start. Crayon on the wall: help wash it off. Toy thrown: it rests on the shelf until after lunch. Two children fighting over a toy: the toy takes a break (see <L to="/situations/#sibling-fighting-now">sibling fighting</L>). In parenting program trials, natural and logical consequences were another of the three techniques linked with stronger effects.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> <L to="/toolbox/#when-then-how">When-then</L> is the positive version: "When your teeth are brushed, then we read." See <L to="/toolbox/#logical-consequences-how">logical consequences</L>.

<h3 id="transitions">Transitions and starting preschool</h3>

Warn before changes: "Two more slides, then we go home." A timer or the <L to="/printables/routine-chart/">routine chart</L> helps. For starting preschool, visit together first if you can, keep goodbyes short and the same every day, say when you will be back in terms your child knows ("after nap"), and don't sneak out. If your child is clingier for a few weeks, add <L to="/toolbox/#connection-time">connection time</L>, not consequences.

With preschool and grandparents in the picture, agree on the few rules that matter most. How well parents cooperate and agree as co-parents is linked with children's adjustment, though the links are small.<Cite id="teubert-pinquart-2010" /> See <L to="/approach/#caregivers-disagree">when caregivers disagree</L> and <L to="/situations/#grandparents-now">grandparents with different rules</L>.

<h2 id="backfires">What backfires</h2>

- **Repeating instructions and warnings.** Say it once, then follow through. Programs that taught consistency, together with time-out, had larger effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" />
- **Attention mostly for misbehavior.** Flip it: more notice for the good moments, less for minor annoyances.
- **Harsh punishment.** The American Academy of Pediatrics says yelling at, shaming or hitting children is minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> In a meta-analysis, corporal punishment was linked with more immediate compliance, but also with more aggression, weaker moral internalization and poorer mental health.<Cite id="gershoff-2002" /> Researchers disagree about occasional mild spanking, but even its defenders found that overly severe physical punishment, or using it as the main method, compared badly with other methods.<Cite id="larzelere-kuhn-2005" /> Both views are set out on <L to="/physical-discipline/#agreement">the physical discipline page</L>.
- **Time-out done badly.** Long, angry, full of lectures, or threatened and never used.
- **Bribes in the middle of a meltdown.** A treat offered to stop a tantrum is not when-then, which is agreed beforehand.
