<h2 id="normal">What's normal at this age</h2>

Between 7 and 10, life fills up with homework, friends and screens. A few things are worth knowing.

**Sleep still matters a lot.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 9 to 12 hours a day for children aged 6 to 12, and getting enough regularly is linked with better attention, behavior, learning and emotional control.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" /> Screens can cut into it. A review of 67 studies of school-aged children and teens found screen time linked with shorter or later sleep in 90% of them, although the studies could not show cause and effect.<Cite id="hale-guan-2015" />

**Lies get more convincing.** In studies of children up to age 8, older children were more likely to lie and to keep a lie going under questioning,<Cite id="talwar-2015" /> and keeping a lie going was linked with understanding what other people think.<Cite id="talwar-lee-2008" /> So catching lies matters less now than making the truth safe to tell.

**Homework is new territory.** In US research, the link between homework and achievement was weaker from kindergarten to grade 6 than in grades 7 to 12, and all the studies had design flaws.<Cite id="cooper-2006-homework" /> At this age, aim for a calm daily habit rather than perfect worksheets.

**It is not too late.** If things have gone off track, two meta-analyses found no evidence that parenting programs work better when started earlier in childhood. They included children up to age 11.<Cite id="gardner-2019-age" />

<h2 id="works">What works, and why</h2>

The core stays the same: warmth, clear expectations and calm, consistent consequences (see the <L to="/approach/#core">best-proven approach</L>). What changes is how much your child takes part in the decisions.

<h3 id="responsibility">Give responsibility, with support</h3>

Hand over real decisions within limits you set, explain your reasons and take your child's point of view seriously. Researchers call this autonomy support and distinguish it from permissiveness: you still hold the limits.<Cite id="joussemet-2008" /> In a meta-analysis of 36 studies, parents' autonomy support was linked with higher achievement, more motivation and engagement at school and better psychological health.<Cite id="vasquez-2016" />

<h3 id="chores">Chores: real jobs, some choice</h3>

Give each child regular jobs that genuinely help the family, such as setting the table, feeding a pet or packing their own school bag, and let them choose some of the jobs. In a meta-analysis of 41 studies, having a choice increased motivation, effort and performance, and the boost to motivation was bigger for children than for adults.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#choices">offering choices</L>.

Should you pay for chores? Researchers disagree. One meta-analysis found that expected, tangible rewards reduced interest in activities, more so for children than for college students.<Cite id="deci-1999" /> Another concluded that rewards are generally not harmful and that rewards for dull tasks can raise motivation.<Cite id="cameron-2001" /> <Mixed /> Both found that praise helped and that promising tangible rewards for something a person already enjoys can backfire. So if you pay, pay for extra jobs, not for reading or drawing.

<h3 id="screens">Screens: a plan, not a nightly fight</h3>

The AAP's newest media policy points out that most digital platforms are designed to boost engagement and profit, not to support children's health.<Cite id="aap-2026-digital-ecosystems" /> So if your child struggles to stop, that is partly by design. Settle the rules ahead of time with a family screen plan: which days, how long, which games and apps, and where devices charge at night. Keep devices out of bedrooms at night; the sleep review above advises limiting screens especially before and during bedtime.<Cite id="hale-guan-2015" /> See <L to="/situations/#screens">screens</L> and <L to="/learning/7-10-years/#sleep-screens">sleep and screens</L>.

<h3 id="homework">Homework: structure, then step back</h3>

Agree on when and where homework happens, keep it the same every day, and stay nearby rather than sitting over every problem. In a research synthesis, parents setting homework rules was more strongly linked with achievement than other kinds of involvement, and training parents to support homework raised completion and cut homework problems in primary school children.<Cite id="patall-2008-homework" /> Doing it for them is different. In a meta-analysis of 448 studies, most kinds of parent involvement in school were linked with better outcomes, but direct homework help was linked with lower achievement. That is a link, not proof of cause.<Cite id="barger-2019" /> A meta-analysis of studies in middle school, a few years older than your child, found the same exception.<Cite id="hill-tyson-2009" /> If homework often ends in tears or takes far longer than the teacher expects, talk to the teacher. See <L to="/situations/#homework">homework</L> and <L to="/learning/7-10-years/#homework">homework on the Encouraging learning page</L>.

<h3 id="friends">Friends and peer problems</h3>

When your child comes home upset about a friend, listen first and hold off on fixing it. Ask what happened, what they want and what they could try, and let them choose. Take aggression seriously, whether it comes from a classmate or a sibling: in a US national sample, sibling aggression and peer aggression were each linked with worse mental health in children and teens.<Cite id="tucker-2013" /> If your child is being hurt, threatened or left out again and again, talk with the school.

<h3 id="family-meetings">Family meetings</h3>

A short weekly meeting gives problems a place to go other than the heat of the moment. Keep it to 15 or 20 minutes: start with something each person appreciates, take one problem at a time, let everyone suggest ideas, agree on one to try and review it the next week. See <L to="/toolbox/#family-meetings">family meetings</L>.

The evidence is for the problem-solving at its heart, and it comes from children with serious behavior problems. In a randomized trial with 7- to 14-year-olds with oppositional defiant disorder, collaborative problem solving worked as well as parent management training: in both groups, about half the children were much improved and no longer met the diagnosis.<Cite id="ollendick-2016" /> In another trial, with 7- to 13-year-olds referred for severe antisocial behavior, problem-solving skills training combined with parent training worked better than either one alone.<Cite id="kazdin-1992-psst" /> For everyday family life, see the meeting as a sensible habit, not a proven treatment.

<h3 id="trust">Earning trust, step by step</h3>

Give more freedom in small, clear steps tied to what your child has shown: "When you've come straight home every day for two weeks, you can stop at the park on the way." Knowing where your child is and who they are with is one of the core skills taught in Parent Management Training-Oregon (PMTO), along with encouragement, limit setting and problem solving.<Cite id="forgatch-kjobli-2016" /> When trust is broken, keep the consequence related and short, and say exactly how it can be earned back. When your child tells you a hard truth, thank them first. See <L to="/situations/#lying">lying</L> and <L to="/toolbox/#privilege-removal">privilege removal</L>.

<h2 id="backfires">What backfires</h2>

**Doing the homework for them.** Direct help was linked with lower achievement (see above).<Cite id="barger-2019" />

**Yelling, insults and shaming.** In a study of mothers and 8- to 12-year-olds in six countries including China, yelling, physical punishment and expressing disappointment were linked with more aggression in children, and several techniques, shaming among them, with more anxiety.<Cite id="gershoff-2010-international" /> A US study of teenagers, a few years older than your child, found that parents' harsh verbal discipline at 13 was linked with more behavior problems and depressive symptoms by 14, and parents' warmth did not soften the link.<Cite id="wang-kenny-2014" />

**Spanking.** Some parents see physical discipline as normal in their culture and therefore harmless. In a study in six countries including China, it was less strongly linked with problems where it was seen as normal, but in every country more physical discipline went with more aggression and anxiety in children aged 6 to 17.<Cite id="lansford-2005" /> The AAP recommends against it.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> A minority of researchers argue that these links mostly reflect other differences between families: in their meta-analysis, after accounting for children's earlier behavior, ordinary spanking explained less than 1% of the remaining differences in each outcome.<Cite id="larzelere-2024" /> The <L to="/physical-discipline/#majority-view">Physical discipline</L> page lays out both sides.
