<h2 id="normal">What's normal at this age</h2>

Starting school asks a lot of a 5- to 7-year-old: sitting still, waiting for a turn, following a teacher's rules and spending long days away from home. If your child holds it together at school and falls apart with you, think tiredness first. A review concludes that stress and lack of sleep both weaken self-control and working memory.<Cite id="diamond-2013" /> In a 2016 consensus statement, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 10 to 13 hours of sleep a day at age 5 and 9 to 12 hours from age 6.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" />

Lying shows up, and it is common. In a study where children aged 3 to 8 were told not to peek at a toy, most peeked and then lied about it. Lying goes hand in hand with new thinking skills: the children's first lies were linked with understanding what another person believes and with self-control.<Cite id="talwar-lee-2008" /> Lies also get more skilled with age. In an experiment with 4- to 8-year-olds, older children were more likely to lie and to keep the lie going under questioning.<Cite id="talwar-2015" /> A lie is a behavior to deal with, not a verdict on your child's character.

Big feelings still take over sometimes, and handling them is a skill that can be taught; one way to start is the <L to="/printables/calm-down-plan/">calm-down plan</L>, filled in together at a calm moment. In a program for siblings aged 4 to 8, children who learned emotional and social skills managed their feelings better, got along better and needed less help from parents to calm down, compared with families on a waiting list.<Cite id="kennedy-kramer-2008" />

From about 7, homework, friends and screens take up more of the day; the School age page covers <L to="/by-age/7-10-years/#normal">what's normal at 7 to 10</L>.

<h2 id="works">What works, and why</h2>

The foundations are the same as in earlier years: warm attention for what you want to see more of, clear expectations, and calm, brief consequences used consistently. Together they are the core of the <L to="/approach/#core">best-proven approach</L>. Regular one-on-one <L to="/toolbox/#connection-time">connection time</L> belongs here too: parenting programs for children up to age 7 that built positive parent-child interaction and emotional communication had larger effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" />

<h3 id="school-days">Make school days predictable</h3>

Keep mornings and evenings in the same order every day, and let a picture list or checklist do the reminding instead of you. Pack the school bag and lay out clothes the night before. After school, plan a soft landing: a snack and some quiet time before questions, homework or activities. A review of 32 publications linked family routines with children's adjustment, while noting weaknesses in the studies, such as convenience samples.<Cite id="fiese-2002" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#routines">routines and visual schedules</L>, <L to="/learning/5-7-years/#sleep-screens">sleep and screens</L>, and <L to="/situations/#bedtime">bedtime battles</L>.

<h3 id="praise">Notice what goes right</h3>

Tell your child what you liked: "You got dressed before breakfast without a reminder." In meta-analyses of parenting program trials, praise was among only 3 of 26 techniques linked with stronger effects on disruptive behavior.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> It does not have to be elaborate. In experiments with 3- to 9-year-olds, praise that named the exact behavior worked no better than a simple "well done."<Cite id="leijten-2016" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#specific-praise">specific praise and attention</L>.

<h3 id="consequences">Let consequences teach</h3>

A natural consequence happens on its own: forget your snack and you are hungry until lunch. When it is safe, let it happen and skip the "I told you so." A logical consequence is one you set that fits the misbehavior: crayon on the wall means helping to scrub it off, and using the tablet past the timer means no tablet for the rest of the day. Natural and logical consequences were also among those 3 techniques linked with stronger effects.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

Taking away a privilege can be a logical consequence too, if it is related and short: screens are done for today, not for a month, and tomorrow is a fresh start. Choose consequences you will actually carry out every time. Programs for children up to age 7 that taught time-out and the importance of consistency had larger effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> For hitting, a brief <L to="/toolbox/#time-out">time-out</L> still fits this age. See <L to="/toolbox/#natural-consequences">natural consequences</L>, <L to="/toolbox/#logical-consequences">logical consequences</L> and <L to="/toolbox/#privilege-removal">privilege removal</L>.

<h3 id="solve-together">Solve problems together</h3>

When the same problem keeps coming back, such as the morning rush or fights over one toy, stop inventing new punishments. At a calm moment, sit down together: each person says what they want, you list ideas without judging them, pick one to try and check back in a few days. In a study of families with 5- to 8-year-olds, mothers trained to mediate their children's disputes could do it, and both mothers and children liked it. The children reasoned, talked about feelings and understood each other's motives more often than children in control families, and mediation especially helped younger siblings solve conflicts. The study looked only at short-term effects.<Cite id="siddiqui-ross-2004" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#problem-solving">solving problems together</L>.

<h3 id="fairness">When it's about fairness</h3>

When you hear "That's not fair!", you don't have to make everything equal. Fair can mean each child gets what they need. Help each child say what they want, then look for a solution both can accept: that is the mediation described above. Step in early when sibling fights get rough. In a national sample, children aged 0 to 9 who had experienced aggression from a sibling in the past year had more mental health distress.<Cite id="tucker-2013" /> See <L to="/situations/#sibling-fighting">sibling fighting</L>.

<h3 id="honesty">Make the truth easy to tell</h3>

When you suspect a lie, don't set a trap by asking a question you already know the answer to. Say what you know, invite the truth and thank your child for it. In an experiment with 372 children aged 4 to 8, children told the truth more often when they heard that the adult would be happy if they told the truth. An appeal to their own standards ("you'll be happy with yourself") worked better when no punishment was expected.<Cite id="talwar-2015" /> Then keep the consequence about putting things right: cleaning up, apologizing, giving back what was taken. See <L to="/situations/#lying">lying</L>.

<h2 id="backfires">What backfires</h2>

**Harsh punishment.** It can look like the way to stop lying, but it can teach children to hide things better. In a natural experiment with 3- and 4-year-olds in West Africa, children at a school that used harsh punishment were more likely than children at a school that did not to lie about peeking at a toy they had been told to leave alone, and they were better at keeping the lie going.<Cite id="talwar-lee-2011" /> It was one study of younger children, but it fits the experiment above, where expecting punishment weakened the appeal to children's own standards of honesty.<Cite id="talwar-2015" />

**Yelling, threats and shaming.** In its 2018 policy statement, the American Academy of Pediatrics advises against them and says harsh methods like these, along with physical punishment, are minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> If you lose your temper, use <L to="/toolbox/#repair">repair after conflict</L>.

**Labels instead of feedback.** "You're so smart" and "You're a bad kid" both judge the child rather than what they did. In two role-play experiments with 5- and 6-year-olds, children who got praise or criticism aimed at themselves showed more helpless reactions after a setback, including blaming themselves, than children who got feedback on how they went about the task.<Cite id="kamins-dweck-1999" />

**Spanking.** A meta-analysis found physical punishment was associated with more immediate compliance, but also with more aggression, weaker moral internalization (taking rules to heart) and poorer mental health.<Cite id="gershoff-2002" /> The AAP's 2018 policy statement also recommends against it.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> A minority of researchers argue that biases and confounds in that meta-analysis limit what it can show about cause, and that its evidence does not justify telling all parents never to spank.<Cite id="baumrind-2002" /> The <L to="/physical-discipline/#majority-view">Physical discipline</L> page sets out both sides.

<h2 id="say">Things you can say</h2>

<Say context="After school">You worked hard all day. Snack first, talk later.</Say>

<Say context="A natural consequence">You were cold at recess without your jacket. What would help you remember tomorrow?</Say>

<Say context="A logical consequence">The tablet was still on after the timer, so it's done for today. You can try again tomorrow.</Say>

<Say context="When you suspect a lie">I already know the cup got broken. I'm not going to shout. I'd be really glad if you told me what happened.</Say>

<Say context="After your child tells the truth">Thank you for telling me. That took courage. Now let's fix it together.</Say>

<Say context="That's not fair!">You both want the window seat. What idea could work for both of you?</Say>

<Say context="Praising effort">You kept trying different ways until it worked.</Say>

<Say context="After you lost your temper">I yelled this morning, and I'm sorry. Let's start again.</Say>
