<h2 id="normal">What's normal at this age</h2>

Between 10 and 12, your child is still a child who needs you, and a young person who needs to be treated as one. Several things change at once.

**Puberty often begins.** With it come body changes, more need for privacy and quicker moods. Ullsperger and Nikolas (2017) pooled 101 articles, and puberty that came earlier than peers' was linked with more emotional and behavior problems in every domain measured, in boys as well as girls, with small effects (d about 0.20).<Cite id="ullsperger-nikolas-2017" /> An early-maturing child is not doing anything wrong; they may simply need more of your calm and more of your time.

**Friends matter more, and peers pull harder.** Steinberg and Monahan (2007) pooled samples of more than 3,600 people aged 10 to 30 and found that the ability to resist peer influence grew steadily between 14 and 18 but showed little growth between 10 and 14.<Cite id="steinberg-monahan-2007" /> Your 10- to 12-year-old, then, is at the age when peers weigh most and the brakes are weakest. The company matters too. Dishion et al. (1999) reviewed the longitudinal and experimental evidence, and it is sobering: "deviancy training" within adolescent friendships predicted increases in delinquency, substance use and violence, and grouping high-risk young adolescents together for interventions made problem behavior worse.<Cite id="dishion-mccord-poulin-1999" /> Knowing who your child's friends are matters more now, not less.

**Arguments get hotter.** The common picture of conflict rising and then falling across adolescence found little support when Laursen et al. (1998) ran meta-analyses of parent-child conflict: the rate of conflict declined from early to mid-adolescence, but its heat increased from early to mid-adolescence and with puberty.<Cite id="laursen-coy-collins-1998" /> Expect fewer but fiercer arguments, and plan for them.

**Lies get more sophisticated.** Evans and Lee (2011) set up an experiment in which 8- to 16-year-olds were left alone with the answers to a test; most peeked, and how well they covered it up, but not whether they lied at all, went with their executive functioning skills.<Cite id="evans-lee-2011" /> Catching every lie is a losing game. Making the truth safe to tell is not.

**Sleep need does not fall.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine still recommends 9 to 12 hours a day for children aged 6 to 12 in its 2016 consensus statement.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" /> Teenagers do sleep less from late childhood on, but Carskadon (2011) concludes from the weight of evidence that their need for sleep does not decline; the lost sleep comes from a mix of biological, psychological and social pressures.<Cite id="carskadon-2011" /> Screens are one of those pressures. Cain and Gradisar (2010) reviewed 36 studies of school-aged children and adolescents and found later bedtimes and shorter sleep to be the outcomes most consistently related to media use.<Cite id="cain-gradisar-2010" />

**Screens and social media.** The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), in its 2016 policy statement for ages 5 to 18, lists benefits (new ideas, knowledge, social contact and support) alongside risks (weight and sleep, inappropriate content and contacts, privacy), and says the evidence does not support a one-size-fits-all approach.<Cite id="aap-2016-school-aged-media" /> On the bigger fear, that technology itself is harming young people's well-being, the research is more reassuring than the headlines. Orben and Przybylski (2019) worked across three large datasets of 355,358 adolescents, and the association between technology use and well-being was negative but small, explaining at most 0.4% of the variation.<Cite id="orben-przybylski-2019" /> Odgers and Jensen (2020) reviewed the field and found mostly correlational studies with a mix of small positive, negative and null associations; they judged the most rigorous findings unlikely to be of practical significance, and cause and effect remain unclear.<Cite id="odgers-jensen-2020" /> That is not a reason to drop limits on sleep, content and contacts, which the pediatric statement still asks for. It is a reason not to panic.

**It is not too late.** Gardner et al. (2019) ran two meta-analyses whose samples went up to age 11 and found no evidence that parenting programs work better when started earlier in childhood.<Cite id="gardner-2019-age" />

<h2 id="works">What works, and why</h2>

The core does not change: warmth, clear expectations and calm, consistent consequences (see the <L to="/approach/#core">best-proven approach</L>). What changes is the balance: more listening, more negotiation, more trust handed over step by step.

<h3 id="warmth-limits">Warmth with limits, and a growing say</h3>

Two large meta-analyses set the frame for this age. Pinquart (2017) pooled 1,435 studies and found that parental warmth, behavioral control (clear rules and follow-through), autonomy granting and an authoritative style (warm and demanding at once) showed very small to small negative links with children's and adolescents' behavior problems, while harsh control and psychological control (guilt, shaming, withdrawing love) showed the strongest positive links, and the links ran in both directions.<Cite id="pinquart-2017-externalizing" /> In a second meta-analysis of 1,015 studies, Pinquart (2017) found the same pattern for anxiety and depression symptoms.<Cite id="pinquart-2017-internalizing" /> So keep the limits, keep the warmth, and give up the guilt trips.

<h3 id="knowing">Know their life because they tell you</h3>

Parents are often told to monitor preteens more closely. The research on what "monitoring" really is points elsewhere. Stattin and Kerr (2000) asked 703 Swedish 14-year-olds and their parents, and what the parents knew came mainly from what their children freely told them; that disclosure, not the parents' tracking, was the source of knowledge most closely linked with less delinquency, and the authors conclude that tracking and surveillance is not the best prescription for parents.<Cite id="stattin-kerr-2000" /> In a second study of 1,186 14-year-olds, Kerr and Stattin (2000) found parents' control efforts linked with good adjustment only once the children's feelings of being controlled, which went with poor adjustment, were set aside.<Cite id="kerr-stattin-2000" /> Both samples were a few years older than your child. Racz and McMahon (2011) reviewed 47 later studies and conclude that inadequate monitoring is a risk factor for conduct problems and that the knowledge behind it is largely driven by what children choose to disclose.<Cite id="racz-mcmahon-2011" />

What this means in practice: be the person your child tells. Keep an unhurried time every day, in the car, over dinner or at bedtime, when you listen more than you talk. React to bad news with calm first. Know their friends and the friends' parents, and make your home the easy place to hang out. Ask where they are going and who with, as a matter of course rather than suspicion. See <L to="/toolbox/#connection-time">connection time</L>.

<h3 id="handing-over">Hand over decisions, step by step</h3>

Trust runs both ways: your child tells you more, and you hand more over. Fuligni and Eccles (1993) surveyed 1,771 sixth and seventh graders: those who believed their parents kept asserting power and did not relax restrictions were higher in an extreme orientation toward peers, and those who saw few chances to take part in family decisions, with none added as they grew, were higher in both that orientation and in seeking advice from peers instead of parents.<Cite id="fuligni-eccles-1993" /> The upside of a growing say shows in the 36 studies Vasquez et al. (2016) pooled: parents' autonomy support was linked with higher achievement, more motivation and engagement at school and better psychological health.<Cite id="vasquez-2016" /> Eccles et al. (1993) argue that some of the dips in motivation seen in early adolescence come from a mismatch between young people's growing need for a say and environments, at school and at home, that give them less of one.<Cite id="eccles-1993-stage-environment" />

So review the rules together. Sort them into three piles: your child's own decisions now (what to wear, how to spend pocket money), shared decisions (bedtime on weekends, which activities) and yours (safety, screens at night, school). Move something from the second pile to the first every few months. See <L to="/toolbox/#choices">offering choices</L>, <L to="/toolbox/#family-meetings">family meetings</L> and, for a child who digs in, <L to="/situations/#defiance-now">defiance and "no"</L>.

<h3 id="screens">Screens: a plan made together</h3>

The pediatric policy statement for ages 5 to 18 asks parents to make a Family Media Use Plan: a balance of media time that fits the child's stage, consistent rules, boundaries on content and on sharing personal information, mentoring, and open conversation about what happens online.<Cite id="aap-2016-school-aged-media" /> Make the plan with your child, not for them, and write it down. Devices charge outside the bedroom at night: later bedtimes and shorter sleep are the most consistent findings on media and sleep,<Cite id="cain-gradisar-2010" /> and Hale and Guan (2015), in a systematic review of 67 studies, advise limiting screens especially before and during bedtime hours.<Cite id="hale-guan-2015" /> Social media is where the plan needs the most detail: which apps, at what age, who they can message, and what to do when something upsets them. See <L to="/situations/#screens">screens</L>.

<h3 id="school">Homework and school: structure, then step back</h3>

Homework starts to count for more. In the US studies Cooper et al. (2006) synthesized, the link between homework and achievement was stronger in grades 7 to 12 than in kindergarten to grade 6, though every one of them had design flaws.<Cite id="cooper-2006-homework" /> Parents' rules about homework had a stronger link with achievement than other kinds of involvement,<Cite id="patall-2008-homework" /> while hands-on help went with lower achievement both across the 448 studies Barger et al. (2019) pooled and in the meta-analysis of middle schoolers by Hill and Tyson (2009); both are links, not proof of cause.<Cite id="barger-2019" /><Cite id="hill-tyson-2009" /> Agree on the routine, stay nearby, and let the work be theirs; see <L to="/situations/#homework">homework</L>. How to study is on the <L to="/learning/10-12-years/#study">Encouraging learning page for this age</L>.

<h3 id="friends">Friends, bullying and peer trouble</h3>

Listen first when friendships go wrong; this is where your child learns to handle people. Take bullying seriously, and know that home plays a part: Lereya et al. (2013) pooled prospective and cross-sectional studies and found children exposed to negative parenting more likely to be bullied, while good communication, a warm relationship, involvement and supervision were protective, with small to moderate effects.<Cite id="lereya-samara-wolke-2013" /> Schools can do something too. Ttofi and Farrington (2011) pooled 44 evaluations and found that school anti-bullying programs cut bullying by 20 to 23% and victimization by 17 to 20%, and that programs with parent meetings and firm discipline were among the more effective ones.<Cite id="ttofi-farrington-2011" /> If your child is being hurt or excluded again and again, go to the school with specifics and ask what program it runs.

<h3 id="honesty">Honesty and trust</h3>

Thank your child for a hard truth before you deal with what they did. Even the 4- to 8-year-olds in the experiment by Talwar et al. (2015) showed why that order matters: expecting punishment weakened the appeal to their own standards of honesty.<Cite id="talwar-2015" /> With a preteen, keep the consequence for the deed related and short, and separate it from the lie: "The lie costs you the phone for the evening. The broken window we fix together." Say exactly how trust is earned back, and notice when it is. See <L to="/situations/#lying">lying</L> and <L to="/toolbox/#privilege-removal">privilege removal</L>.

<h3 id="problem-solving">Family meetings and solving problems together</h3>

A weekly meeting gives arguments somewhere to go. For problems that keep coming back, work through them together: define the problem, hear both sides, list options, pick one to try, review it next week. The evidence comes from clinic trials with children with serious behavior problems. A collaborative problem-solving approach worked as well as parent management training for 7- to 14-year-olds with oppositional defiant disorder in the randomized trial by Ollendick et al. (2015), with about half the children in both groups much improved,<Cite id="ollendick-2016" /> and in a trial with 7- to 13-year-olds referred for severe antisocial behavior, Kazdin et al. (1992) found problem-solving skills training combined with parent training worked better than either alone.<Cite id="kazdin-1992-psst" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#problem-solving">solving problems together</L>.

<h3 id="secondary-school">The move to secondary school</h3>

The jump to middle school (or its equivalent where you live) brings more teachers, more homework and a new social world at exactly the moment your child wants more independence. Researchers describe the risk as a mismatch: a young person who needs more say meets a bigger, more controlling environment.<Cite id="eccles-1993-stage-environment" /> At home you can supply the fit: keep routines steady in the first term, ask about the new school every day without interrogating, give more responsibility for homework and getting ready, and hold the family meeting weekly until things settle.

The six By age pages end here, but the core does not: warmth, clear expectations and calm, consistent consequences, with more of the say handed to your child each year. For your child's motivation across the same move, see the <L to="/learning/10-12-years/#motivation">Encouraging learning page for this age</L>; when steady effort is not easing things after several weeks, the Best-proven approach page says what to check <L to="/approach/#not-working">when it is not working</L>.

<h2 id="backfires">What backfires</h2>

**Harsh control and guilt.** Of all the parenting dimensions in those 1,435 studies, harsh control and psychological control (guilt, shaming, withdrawing love) had the strongest links with behavior problems, and the links ran both ways: behavior problems also went with harsher parenting later.<Cite id="pinquart-2017-externalizing" /> The 6-country study of mothers and 8- to 12-year-olds by Gershoff et al. (2010), China among the countries, linked yelling and expressing disappointment with more aggression and shaming with more anxiety.<Cite id="gershoff-2010-international" /> Wang and Kenny (2014) followed 13-year-olds in the US, a little older than your child, and linked harsh verbal discipline with more behavior problems and depressive symptoms a year later; parental warmth did not soften the link.<Cite id="wang-kenny-2014" />

**Checking up instead of being told.** Recall Kerr and Stattin (2000): parents' control efforts were linked with good adjustment only after children's feelings of being controlled, which went with poor adjustment, were set aside.<Cite id="kerr-stattin-2000" /> Reading a diary or scrolling a phone behind your child's back buys a little information at the price of the disclosure that protects them most.

**Tightening the rules, or giving no say.** Sixth and seventh graders who saw their parents hold on to power and offer no growing part in decisions were more likely to turn sharply toward peers.<Cite id="fuligni-eccles-1993" />

**Lectures and nagging.** Not a research finding, just a pattern every parent of a preteen recognizes: the longer the speech, the less of it lands. One sentence, then stop.

**Spanking.** However normal it was seen to be, more physical discipline went with more aggression and anxiety in children aged 6 to 17 in the study by Lansford et al. (2005) across 6 countries including China.<Cite id="lansford-2005" /> The AAP (2018) position is the same: its policy statement on discipline recommends against it.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> A minority of researchers, Larzelere et al. (2024) among them, argue that such links are largely due to other differences between families.<Cite id="larzelere-2024" /> The <L to="/physical-discipline/#majority-view">Physical discipline</L> page lays out both sides.

**Screens in the bedroom.** Across the sleep studies, later bedtimes and shorter sleep are the findings most consistently tied to media use in school-aged children and adolescents.<Cite id="cain-gradisar-2010" />
