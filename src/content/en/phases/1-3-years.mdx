<h2 id="normal">What's normal at this age</h2>

Most of what worries parents at this age turns out to be common, and the numbers below say how common. One practical note first: expect to repeat the same few limits many, many times.

<h3 id="hitting-biting">Hitting, biting and pushing</h3>

If your toddler hits, bites or pushes, you have plenty of company, and it is not a verdict on your child or your parenting. Tremblay et al. (2004) followed 572 families and found that most toddlers used some physical aggression; only 28% used little or none.<Cite id="tremblay-2004" /> Among more than 2,000 children in another study, physical aggression was already present at 12 months, was more common at 24 and 36 months, and declined from the third birthday onward.<Cite id="alink-2006" /> Côté et al. (2006), following more than 10,000 Canadian children, found that most used it occasionally or rarely as toddlers, and rarely or almost never by the preteen years.<Cite id="cote-2006" /> What you are looking at is something to teach, calmly and many times.

<h3 id="tantrums">Tantrums</h3>

Tantrums are common in early childhood: nearly all (83.7%) of the preschoolers in one large community study had them sometimes, but only 8.6% had them every day.<Cite id="wakschlag-2012" /> Ordinary tantrums tend to follow frustration in situations you could predict; the kind worth raising with a professional are unpredictable, very long or destructive.<Cite id="wakschlag-2012" /> See <L to="/situations/#tantrums-help">tantrums: when to get help</L>.

<h3 id="sleep">Sleep</h3>

Sleep belongs on this list. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 11-14 hours of sleep in 24 hours for 1- and 2-year-olds, naps included, and regularly getting the recommended amount is linked with better attention, behavior and emotional control.<Cite id="paruthi-2016" /> If the hardest moments cluster before naps or late in the day, look at sleep first (see <L to="/learning/1-3-years/#sleep-screens">sleep and screens</L>).

<h3 id="when-to-ask">When to ask for help</h3>

If you are worried about how your child talks, plays or connects with people, raise it at a well-child visit. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends routine autism screening at 18 and 24 months.<Cite id="hyman-2020-autism" /> If hitting or tantrums seem far more intense than other children's, see <L to="/approach/#developmental-differences">developmental differences</L>.

From about 3, rules, praise and calm consequences can do more of the work; the Preschoolers page covers <L to="/by-age/3-5-years/#normal">what's normal at 3 to 5</L>.

<h2 id="works">What works, and why</h2>

The approach behind the best-tested parenting programs is the same at every age: warm attention for the behavior you want, clear instructions, ignoring small stuff, brief and calm consequences, and steady follow-through (see <L to="/approach/#core">the core approach</L>). It holds up under testing: Comer et al. (2013) pooled 36 controlled trials with young children (average age 4.7) and found that behavioral treatments had the largest effects on disruptive behavior.<Cite id="comer-2013" />

<h3 id="limits">A few firm limits</h3>

Keep the list of real limits short and make them never move: no hurting people or animals, car seat buckled, hold hands near the road. Everything else can bend, mealtimes included (see <L to="/situations/#mealtime">mealtime struggles</L>). Consistency is not a small thing: when Kaminski et al. (2008) analyzed parenting programs, the ones that taught parents consistency, together with time-out, had larger effects.<Cite id="kaminski-2008" /> When your toddler keeps going back to the same off-limits thing, <L to="/situations/#testing-limits-now">my toddler keeps testing limits</L> has a plan for making the limit hold.

Say what you want, not only what you don't: "Walk" instead of "Don't run". A short, firm "Stop" is fine. In a systematic review of studies with children aged 1.5 to 11, Owen et al. (2012) found that reprimands consistently increased compliance.<Cite id="owen-2012" /> Firm is not harsh, though. The American Academy of Pediatrics says, in its 2018 policy statement, that yelling at or shaming children is minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#clear-expectations-how">clear expectations and instructions</L>.

<h3 id="redirect-and-praise">Redirect, then praise</h3>

Offer something your child is allowed to do that meets the same urge: "Blocks stay on the floor. You can throw this ball outside." Redirecting and setting limits are among the alternatives to punishment the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends in its 2018 policy statement.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> When your child takes the new option, notice it: "Wow, you threw that ball so far!" That noticing matters more than it looks: across parenting program trials, praise was one of only 3 of 26 techniques linked with stronger effects on disruptive behavior.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#redirection-how">redirection</L>, and <L to="/toolbox/#specific-praise-how">specific praise and attention</L>; the Encouraging learning page adds <L to="/learning/1-3-years/#praise">praise: notice the effort</L>.

<h3 id="choices">Choices</h3>

Offer two choices you are happy with either way: "Red cup or blue cup?" "Walk to the car or hop like a bunny?" Don't ask "Do you want to get dressed?" when the answer has to be yes. Patall et al. (2008) pooled 41 studies with children and adults and found that having choices boosted motivation and effort, and boosted motivation more in children than in adults.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" /> Those studies measured motivation, not obedience, so use choices to get things moving, not to stop misbehavior. See <L to="/toolbox/#choices-how">offering choices</L>, and for the learning side of this, <L to="/learning/1-3-years/#motivation">motivation: let them do it</L>.

<h3 id="no-stage">When "no" is the answer to everything, or nothing is</h3>

- Ask fewer yes-or-no questions: "Bath time," not "Do you want a bath?"
- Use <L to="/toolbox/#when-then-how">when-then</L>: "When your shoes are on, then we go to the park."
- Make it a game: race to the car, sing the clean-up song.
- Let small things go. Mismatched socks are not a limit.

Supporting your child's autonomy, their wish to have some say, is not the same as being permissive. Joussemet et al. (2008) keep the two apart in their review and report that observational studies link this kind of autonomy support with toddlers taking rules on as their own.<Cite id="joussemet-2008" /> See <L to="/situations/#defiance-now">defiance and "no"</L>.

If the answer is not "no" but silence, and your toddler carries on as if you had not spoken, see <L to="/situations/#ignores-me-now">my toddler ignores me</L>: get close, get their attention, say it once, wait, then guide them through it.

<h3 id="calming-down">Helping your toddler calm down</h3>

During a tantrum:

1. Keep everyone safe. Move your child, or move things away from them.
2. Stay close, or a step back if touch makes it worse. Few words, low voice: "I'm here. You're really mad."
3. Hold the limit that started it. Don't argue, explain or bargain. Wait.
4. When it passes, reconnect with a hug or a drink of water, and sum it up in one sentence: "You were so angry we had to leave. Now you're calm."

If the tantrum is about getting something you said no to, keep the no and give your full attention again once it is over. That is <L to="/toolbox/#planned-ignoring-how">planned ignoring</L> of the tantrum, not of your child.

Staying calm and close while your child settles is sometimes called co-regulation, and as a tool it is often called <L to="/toolbox/#time-in-how">time-in</L>. It is meant to help a child connect with you, share feelings and learn to calm down, but the research is early. Holden et al. (2022) ran a small pilot study in which mothers of 3- to 5-year-olds rated it easy to use and effective, and they note there are few studies of it.<Cite id="holden-2022" /> See also <L to="/situations/#public-meltdowns-now">public meltdowns</L>.

<h3 id="after-hitting">After hitting or biting</h3>

1. Stop it at once. Hold the hand or move your child away: "I won't let you bite."
2. Turn first to the child who was hurt.
3. Show what to do instead: stroke gently, say "My turn!", or bite a teether.
4. Watch for patterns, such as tiredness, crowds or one toy everyone wants, and step in earlier next time.

If hitting keeps happening, see <L to="/approach/#aggression">hitting and other aggression</L>, and <L to="/situations/#hitting-biting-prevent">hitting and biting</L>.

<h3 id="bedtime-routine">Bedtime routine</h3>

Toddlers aged 18-36 months whose mothers started a consistent bedtime routine in a randomized study fell asleep faster and woke less at night, and their mothers' mood improved.<Cite id="mindell-2009" /> See <L to="/toolbox/#routines-how">routines and visual schedules</L>, and <L to="/situations/#bedtime-prevent">bedtime battles</L>.

<h2 id="backfires">What backfires</h2>

- **Giving in once the screaming starts.** If the limit was worth setting, keep it. You can be kind about the feeling and still not hand over the cookie.
- **Explaining, bargaining and lecturing mid-tantrum.** Save the words for when your child is calm.
- **Hitting, slapping or biting back "so they know how it feels".** The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against all corporal punishment in its 2018 policy statement.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> Spanking at age 1 was linked, in a study of nearly 2,800 families, with a higher chance of later child protective services involvement, and the authors advise professionals to counsel parents not to spank infants and toddlers.<Cite id="lee-2014-cps" /> Researchers disagree about whether occasional mild spanking adds harm. Larzelere et al. (2024), who are in the minority, argue that harmful-looking results for ordinary spanking are likely due to other differences between families that studies could not fully control.<Cite id="larzelere-2024" /> Both sides, and what they agree on, are on <L to="/physical-discipline/#disagreement">the physical discipline page</L>.
- **Yelling, threats and shaming.** The same 2018 policy statement from the American Academy of Pediatrics advises against threats and shaming, and says yelling at or shaming children is minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" />
- **Too many rules, and warnings you won't carry out.** Say less, and do what you say.
- **A phone to end every meltdown.** In its 2016 statement on young children and media, the American Academy of Pediatrics discouraged screens other than video chat before 18 months. From 18 to 24 months, it advised parents who introduce media to choose high-quality content and use it with their child.<Cite id="aap-2016-media" /> See <L to="/situations/#screens-prevent">screens</L>.

<h2 id="say">Things you can say</h2>

Short, calm and the same each time. Say it once, then act.

<Say context="Your child hits">I won't let you hit. Hitting hurts. You can stomp your feet instead.</Say>

<Say context="Your child bites">Ouch! Biting hurts. If you need to bite, bite this.</Say>

<Say context="A standoff at bath time">Do you want to walk to the bath or ride on my back?</Say>

<Say context="Leaving the house">Shoes on first, then we go out.</Say>

<Say context="During a tantrum">You're really angry. I'm right here. I'll wait with you.</Say>

<Say context="Holding the limit">You can be mad. The answer is still no.</Say>

<Say context="After the storm">That was a big feeling. You calmed down. Hug?</Say>

<Say context="Catching the good stuff">You gave the car back. That was kind.</Say>
