- "Move first, talk second. Step in and block the reach, lift your child away, or put your body between your child and the pet. Calm hands, low voice, no chasing. See [how to step in physically, calmly](/physical-discipline/#safety-holds)."
- "Say one short rule, in the same words every time, and then stop talking: \"Gentle with the dog,\" or \"Cups stay on the table.\" No lecture and no second warning."
- "Give the same short consequence straight away: a minute or two in a safe spot beside you that your child can't leave, such as a playpen or a corner behind a safety gate, where you can see each other. You move the plant, and your child goes back for the leaves. You say no, and they hold your eye and do it anyway. It looks like defiance. It works better to treat it as a question your child keeps asking: does this limit hold every time, or only sometimes? Everything above is built to make the answer the same, every time, from everyone.

**Why toddlers keep testing.** Saying no is so ordinary at this age that Wierson and Forehand (1994) call it a normal characteristic of the developing 2- to 3-year-old.<Cite id="wierson-forehand-1994" /> Self-control is still being built: when Vaughn et al. (1984) assessed 72 children between 18 and 30 months, the ability to wait for something attractive and the ability to follow a mother's directions both grew with age.<Cite id="vaughn-kopp-krakow-1984" /> Rules start small, too. In the families Gralinski and Kopp (1993) followed, mothers' earliest rules centered on safety, children kept best to the rules about safety and other people's things, and the shift from being held to a rule to holding it themselves came gradually.<Cite id="gralinski-kopp-1993" /> A toddler who keeps going back is somewhere in the middle of that shift.

**Prevention does half the work.** Every tempting thing within reach is a no you will have to enforce many times over, so take away the ones you can. Holden (1983) watched 24 mothers shopping with their toddlers, and while all of them sometimes reacted after the fact, the mothers who often headed off trouble in advance, by turning the child's attention elsewhere, had children with fewer undesired behaviors.<Cite id="holden-1983" /> Families can make these changes stick: in the Cochrane review by Kendrick et al. (2012), home safety programs got more families storing medicines and cleaning products out of reach, though the evidence that they cut injuries was weaker.<Cite id="kendrick-2012-cochrane" /> A gate still needs a grown-up nearby. Mothers in the diary study by Morrongiello et al. (2006) recorded how they supervised their 2- to 5-year-olds, and alongside the child's temperament, supervision helped predict the injuries that needed medical care.<Cite id="morrongiello-2006-supervision" />

**Hands first, then one short line.** A "no" called across the room leaves the next move to your child. Walking over and blocking the reach, or lifting your child away, takes it out of their hands in seconds, and it is a safety action, not a punishment (see <L to="/physical-discipline/#safety-holds">how to step in physically, calmly</L>). Keep it calm. Houck and LeCuyer-Maus (2004) observed mothers setting limits when their children were 12, 24 and 36 months old: a teaching-based pattern gave the most consistently positive results at 36 months, while a power-based pattern went with poorer self-control at 5.<Cite id="houck-lecuyer-maus-2004" /> Then say the rule once, in the same few words. A short reprimand is not harsh, and across the studies of children aged 1.5 to 11 that Owen et al. (2012) reviewed, reprimands consistently increased compliance.<Cite id="owen-2012" /> Long explanations add little at this age: Wilder et al. (2010) gave six young children reasons before or after an instruction, and the reasons helped none of them.<Cite id="wilder-2010-rationales" /> If your child ignores "stop" in general, not only around one tempting thing, see <L to="/situations/#ignores-me-now">my toddler ignores me</L>.

**The same short consequence, enforced.** A brief break from the action is one of the best-tested consequences there is. Leijten et al. (2018) pooled 19 experiments and found that time-out for not cooperating robustly increased children's compliance, though most children in those studies were between 3 and 8.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> Hence a short break beside you for a younger toddler, and a proper time-out from about 3. The details decide whether it works. When Riley et al. (2017) surveyed 401 parents at check-ups, about three in four used time-out, and most of them did it in ways the evidence does not support.<Cite id="riley-2017-timeout-survey" /> Drayton et al. (2014) went through 102 web pages on time-out, found none that got every part right, and note that dropping any single part makes it less effective.<Cite id="drayton-2014-timeout-guidance" /> Staying in the break is one of those parts. In a study of 24 preschoolers, Bean and Roberts (1981) found that compliance rose under both versions of time-out they tested, but when children could leave whenever they chose, the gain was judged too small to matter; in the other version, a spanking was used to prevent early escapes.<Cite id="bean-roberts-1981" /> Day and Roberts (1983) then compared that spanking with a barrier as the response when 16 noncompliant preschoolers left time-out, found the two equally effective, and concluded that enforcing a minimum time-out was the critical part.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" /> At home, a playpen or a safety gate across a doorway does the barrier's job, with you in sight and silent.

**When your child runs straight back.** This is the moment that decides whether the testing stops. A limit that holds only some of the time is still worth testing, and it only takes one adult laughing it off, or a consequence that arrives on the fourth try. Gardner (1989) watched families at home and found that mothers of preschoolers with conduct problems failed to follow through on their demands in more of their conflicts than other mothers did, and the less consistent the mother, the more conflict there was in the family.<Cite id="gardner-1989-inconsistency" /> So a quick return gets a quicker answer: no warning, straight to the break, and the activity is over for the day. Closing it like that is a logical consequence, one of only three techniques Leijten et al. (2019) found linked with stronger effects in parenting program trials.<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> It works only if every adult does it. Teubert and Pinquart (2010) pooled 59 studies and found that how well parents cooperate and agree is linked with how children are doing, small effects that held after each parent's own parenting was taken into account.<Cite id="teubert-pinquart-2010" />

**Pets need a plan of their own.** A pet can answer back, so this part has no exceptions. Most of the research is on dogs. Schalamon et al. (2006) reviewed ten years of dog bites to children in one district and found the yearly rate highest at age 1, with 82% of the dogs familiar to the child; half of the injuries were to the face, head or neck.<Cite id="schalamon-2006-dogs" /> Among 100 children treated in emergency departments, Kahn et al. (2003) found that those bitten at home had a median age of 5 and no adult supervising them at the time.<Cite id="kahn-2003-dogs" /> A calm dog is no guarantee either. When Reisner et al. (2007) studied dogs brought to a veterinary behavior clinic after biting a child, two thirds of those with a known history had never bitten a child before, and children under 6 were most often bitten while the dog was guarding food or other things it valued.<Cite id="reisner-2007-dogs" /> Even friendly contact counts: Arhant et al. (2017) point out that at these ages petting, as well as interfering with the dog's things, commonly comes before a bite from the family dog, and they call for prevention aimed at parents from the very start.<Cite id="arhant-2017-dogs" /> So keep the pet's food and bed off-limits and give it a retreat your child cannot enter. When play turns rough, your body goes between them before anything else.

**Repair, and practice for the gentle version.** After the break, let your child help put things right, wiping up the spill or gathering the soil, with you alongside. Toddlers manage more of this than you might expect. Drummond et al. (2017) led 32 toddlers, about two and a half on average, to believe they had broken an adult's toy: some owned up and tried to fix it, others kept away from the adult, and the ones who tried to fix it later helped an adult in distress faster and more often.<Cite id="drummond-2017-repair" /> The motive changes around the third birthday. Vaish et al. (2016) found that 2-year-olds repaired harm whoever had caused it, out of sympathy, while 3-year-olds repaired most when they had caused it themselves.<Cite id="vaish-2016-guilt" /> Keep repair practical and warm, never a shaming. With a pet, practice the gentle version with your hand over your child's, a few strokes at a time. In the studies Shen et al. (2017) pooled on teaching children how to behave with dogs, instruction with a live dog did the most for behavior, though the quality of the evidence was poor.<Cite id="shen-2017-dog-safety" /> Then notice the gentle moments out loud. Praise and other positive reinforcement were among the three techniques linked with stronger effects in the trials Leijten et al. (2019) pooled.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

**What about spanking?** Some parents wonder whether a smack would make the stop stick. Most of the research points the other way, and part of it concerns this exact behavior: when Power and Chapieski (1986) observed 18 toddlers at home, those whose mothers relied on physical punishment were more likely to ignore their mothers' prohibitions and to handle breakable objects.<Cite id="power-chapieski-1986" /> Gershoff and Grogan-Kaylor (2016) pooled 111 effect sizes from 160,927 children, and 13 of 17 mean effects linked spanking with a higher risk of detrimental outcomes,<Cite id="gershoff-grogan-kaylor-2016" /> and in its 2018 policy statement the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that adults not hit children at all.<Cite id="aap-2018-discipline" /> A minority of researchers, Larzelere et al. (2024) among them, point to large effects in clinic programs for defiant young children that used spanking to enforce time-out.<Cite id="larzelere-2024" /> In the direct comparison by Day and Roberts (1983), though, a barrier worked as well as spanking did.<Cite id="day-roberts-1983" /> <L to="/physical-discipline/#disagreement">Physical discipline</L> sets out both sides, and looks at studies from China on <L to="/physical-discipline/#school-success">whether children who were hit grow up more disciplined</L>. For this problem, the stronger tools are the ones on this page: your body between your child and the thing, and a break that is enforced every time.

**How long it takes.** When a behavior stops working, it can flare up before it fades. Lerman and Iwata (1995) went through 113 sets of data from applied behavior research and found such a burst in about a quarter of cases: 12% when withholding the payoff was combined with other procedures, 36% when it was used alone.<Cite id="lerman-iwata-1995" /> That is one more reason to pair every stop with a gentle alternative and plenty of praise. Over the months, development does much of the rest. Kochanska et al. (2001) followed 108 children from 14 to 45 months: at every age, leaving something attractive alone came more easily than a tedious "do", and the children who went along wholeheartedly were the ones who kept the rules when no one was watching.<Cite id="kochanska-coy-murray-2001" /> Measure progress by fewer returns in a session, then fewer sessions that end early, not by a toddler who never tests again.

**At 3 to 5.** From about 3, the break becomes a short <L to="/toolbox/#time-out">time-out</L>, and a reason can follow once everyone is calm. If the testing turns into flat refusals, <L to="/situations/#defiance-now">defiance and "no"</L> takes over from here, and if it turns into hitting or biting, see <L to="/situations/#hitting-biting-now">hitting and biting</L>.
