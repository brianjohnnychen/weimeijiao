now:
- "Go to your child, get down to their level and get their attention first: say their name, touch their arm, wait for them to look. The order matters. Schaffer and Crook (1980) found that with 15- and 24-month-olds a mother's instruction was most likely to succeed as part of a sequence, first drawing the child's attention to the object, then asking for the action.[[cite:schaffer-crook-1980]]"
- "Give one short instruction as a statement, not a question, and show it with a gesture: \"Cup on the table,\" with a tap on the table. See [clear expectations and instructions](/toolbox/#clear-expectations)."
- "Then wait a few seconds, quietly. Toddlers need a moment to switch from what they are doing. Don't repeat yourself yet, and don't add more words."
- "If nothing happens, say it once more with a when-then or a two-way choice: \"When the cup is on the table, then we go outside,\" or \"Do you want to carry the cup, or shall I?\" See [when-then](/toolbox/#when-then) and [offering choices](/toolbox/#choices)."
- "Still nothing? Guide them through it, hands on and calm: put your hand over theirs and move the cup together, or take their hand and walk to the bathroom together. Wilder and Atwell (2006) tried this with six typically developing preschoolers: an instruction followed by progressively firmer prompts, ending in gentle physical guidance, reduced noncompliance for four of them.[[cite:wilder-atwell-2006]]"
- "Thank them the moment it is done, even if you helped: \"Thank you, cup on the table!\" Then let it go: no lecture, no sigh, no grudge."
- "If what they are ignoring is a \"stop\" rather than a \"do\", move them or the object instead of repeating yourself, and offer what they can do: \"Crayons are for paper. Here is the paper.\" See [redirection](/toolbox/#redirection). If they keep going back to it, see [my toddler keeps testing limits](/situations/#testing-limits-now)."

prevent:
- "Give fewer instructions, and make each one count: one at a time, up close, said once. A toddler who hears a stream of \"come here\" from across the room learns that most of them don't mean anything. See [follow-through](/approach/#follow-through)."
- "Fill the day with attention for what you want to see: a smile, a word, a \"thank you\" when they come the first time. Across the two meta-analyses of parenting program trials by Leijten et al. (2019), praise and other positive reinforcement were among the few techniques linked with stronger effects on difficult behavior.[[cite:leijten-2019]] See [specific praise and attention](/toolbox/#specific-praise)."
- "Spend a few minutes a day following your child's lead, with no instructions at all. When Kaminski et al. (2008) pooled 77 evaluations of parenting programs, the ones that taught positive parent-child interaction had larger effects.[[cite:kaminski-2008]] See [connection time](/toolbox/#connection-time)."
- "Move in before trouble builds: steer them toward what they can do instead of calling across the room to stop. See [redirection](/toolbox/#redirection)."
- "Let the small stuff go. Whining, dawdling and silly noises get nothing from you; coming when called gets your full attention. See [planned ignoring](/toolbox/#planned-ignoring)."
- "Keep the big transitions the same every day, with a warning before each one: \"Two more slides, then shoes.\" See [routines and visual schedules](/toolbox/#routines)."

You say your toddler's name and nothing happens. You ask again, louder, and they carry on stacking cups as if you were not in the room. Before you decide that your child is willful or that you have lost your authority, know two things. This is what toddlers do. And the steps above change it more than volume does.

**Why toddlers tune you out.** Two things are growing at once. The first is autonomy: a 2-year-old is discovering that they are a separate person with plans of their own, and carrying on with those plans is the simplest way to say so. Crockenberg and Litman (1990) studied 95 two-year-olds and treated this growing autonomy as a competence, measured through three kinds of behavior at once, defying, complying and asserting themselves, which is a useful reminder that doing it their own way is part of becoming a separate person, not only a problem to fix.<Cite id="crockenberg-litman-1990" /> The second is self-control, which is still under construction: when Vaughn et al. (1984) assessed 72 children aged 18 to 30 months, the ability to wait for something attractive and the ability to follow a mother's directions to tidy up both increased with age, and the authors conclude that self-control emerges and settles during the second and third years of life.<Cite id="vaughn-kopp-krakow-1984" /> What you are asking for matters too. Kochanska et al. (2001) followed 108 children from 14 to 45 months, and "do" requests, such as keeping at a tedious task like tidying up, were much harder for children at every age than "don't" requests, such as leaving something attractive alone.<Cite id="kochanska-coy-murray-2001" /> "Come here" and "put it away" are exactly the instructions a toddler is least equipped to follow.

**Ignoring is a stage.** When Kuczynski et al. (1987) watched 1½- to 3½-year-olds at home, passive noncompliance, the quiet carrying-on that parents experience as being ignored, decreased with age, as did outright defiance, while negotiating increased; children whose mothers reasoned and made suggestions tended to negotiate, and more direct maternal strategies went with defiance.<Cite id="kuczynski-1987-noncompliance" /> Your toddler is not becoming more defiant. They are on their way to becoming a child who argues back, which, oddly enough, is progress.

**Why the steps work.** Getting close and getting attention comes first because a toddler handles one thing at a time, which is what Schaffer and Crook (1980) saw in their 15- and 24-month-olds: instructions worked best as part of an attention-then-action sequence.<Cite id="schaffer-crook-1980" /> Radley and Dart (2016) reviewed 42 studies and concluded that delivering an instruction effectively, and starting with a few easy requests before a harder one, may be considered evidence-based ways to increase cooperation.<Cite id="radley-dart-2016" /> In the programs that teach it, an effective instruction is given up close, once you have your child's attention, as a short statement followed by a short wait; the parents Mandal et al. (2000) trained in it raised cooperation in children who had been following fewer than half of first-time instructions,<Cite id="mandal-2000-eid" /> and with 5- and 6-year-olds, Ford et al. (2001) found that it alone raised instruction-following by 21% to 44% above baseline.<Cite id="ford-2001-compliance-training" />

**Why hands-on, calm and brief.** Guiding your child's hands through the task when they do not respond is the toddler version of follow-through, and it keeps the moment short and calm. Its evidence is mixed: Wilder and Atwell (2006) saw it work for four of six preschoolers,<Cite id="wilder-atwell-2006" /> and Wilder et al. (2010) for one of six young children, where what worked for the rest was getting something they wanted once they had complied, which is when-then.<Cite id="wilder-2010-rationales" /> The same six children also heard reasons, before or after the instruction, and that helped none of them, so save the reasons for a calm moment later.<Cite id="wilder-2010-rationales" /> The calm, teaching tone matters in the long run: Houck and LeCuyer-Maus (2004) observed mothers setting limits when their children were 12, 24 and 36 months old, and a teaching-based pattern gave the most consistently positive results, while a power-based pattern went with poorer self-control at age 5.<Cite id="houck-lecuyer-maus-2004" /> With a 1-year-old, the gentle route also beats punishment: when Power and Chapieski (1986) watched 18 toddlers aged 12 to 15 months at home, the children of mothers who relied on physical punishment were more likely to ignore their mothers' prohibitions.<Cite id="power-chapieski-1986" />

**Why thanks and choices.** Thanking your child right away keeps your attention flowing to the behavior you want. Praise on its own has mixed results for compliance,<Cite id="owen-2012" /> but in parenting program trials it was among the few techniques linked with stronger effects,<Cite id="leijten-2019" /> and the aim is wider than obedience anyway: among the 108 children Kochanska et al. (2001) followed to 45 months, those who went along wholeheartedly rather than only under pressure were the ones who later kept the rules when alone.<Cite id="kochanska-coy-murray-2001" /> Choices get things moving because they give the growing autonomy a say: Patall et al. (2008) pooled 41 studies, and having a choice raised motivation, more so in children than in adults.<Cite id="patall-2008-choice" />

**At 3 to 5.** The same steps work, with a few more words allowed: two-step instructions, three choices, and a reason once the thing is done rather than before. A short <L to="/toolbox/#time-out">time-out</L> becomes an option for outright refusals: Leijten et al. (2018) pooled the experimental studies, and time-out for not following an instruction reliably increased cooperation.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> When your preschooler's silence turns into "no", the <L to="/situations/#defiance-now">defiance guide</L> takes over from here.
