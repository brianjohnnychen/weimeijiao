Refusals wear parents down, but you have more influence than it feels like in the moment. Research points to what you can change: how you give the instruction, and what happens after it.

How you ask: having reviewed 42 studies, Radley and Dart (2016) concluded that delivering instructions effectively, and starting with a few easy requests your child is likely to follow before a harder one, may be considered evidence-based ways to increase cooperation.<Cite id="radley-dart-2016" /> What happens next: across the experiments Leijten et al. (2018) pooled, time-out and briefly ignoring the child after a refusal both reliably increased cooperation.<Cite id="compliance-plos-2018" /> Praise on its own had mixed results for getting children to follow an instruction,<Cite id="owen-2012" /> but it still earns its place: in the 2 meta-analyses of parenting program trials by Leijten et al. (2019), praise and other positive reinforcement, along with natural and logical consequences, were the techniques linked with stronger program effects on difficult behavior.<Cite id="leijten-2019" />

For children 7 and older whose defiance is persistent, <L to="/toolbox/#problem-solving">solving problems together</L> is another option with trial evidence. Ollendick et al. (2015) randomized 134 children aged 7 to 14 with a diagnosis of oppositional defiant disorder, and Collaborative and Proactive Solutions, an approach in which adult and child work out solutions together, worked as well as parent management training: after either treatment, about half the children no longer met the diagnosis and were much improved, compared with none on the waiting list.<Cite id="ollendick-2016" />

**At 10 to 12.** Flat refusals turn into negotiation. Across the studies Laursen et al. (1998) pooled, arguments get less frequent but more heated from early to mid-adolescence, though the finding on heat rests on few studies,<Cite id="laursen-coy-collins-1998" /> and in the families Fuligni and Eccles (1993) followed, children who saw no growing say in family decisions turned more sharply toward peers.<Cite id="fuligni-eccles-1993" /> Give a real say in the rules, keep the ones that matter, and take the argument to the family meeting instead of the doorway. See <L to="/by-age/10-12-years/#handing-over">handing over decisions on the Preteens page</L>.
