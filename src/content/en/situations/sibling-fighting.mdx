now:
- "If anyone is being hurt, step in and separate them first. Safety comes before fairness."
- "Give each child a calm spot apart for a few minutes, with no blame yet."
- "Don't try to work out who started it in the heat of the moment."
- "If they're fighting over something, put it out of reach for now: “The truck takes a break until you two have a plan.”"
- "If someone hit, use your usual consequence for hitting, whoever did it. See [hitting and biting](/situations/#hitting-biting-now)."
- "Once everyone is calm, sit down for [solving problems together](/toolbox/#problem-solving): each child says what happened and how they feel, then they suggest ideas and pick one to try."
say:
- "I'm not going to decide who's right. I'm going to help you both work it out."
- "You both want the same toy. That's a tough problem. What ideas do you have?"
- "Tell me what happened. Then it's your sister's turn, and nobody interrupts."
- "In our family we don't hurt each other. You can be angry and still keep your hands to yourself."
- "You worked that out yourselves. That was hard, and you did it."
prevent:
- "Give each child some one-on-one [connection time](/toolbox/#connection-time), even 10 minutes a day."
- "Notice them getting along: “You two built that together.” Meta-analyses of parenting program trials found praise among the few techniques linked with stronger effects on disruptive behavior.[[cite:leijten-2019]]"
- "Agree on a few family rules together at a [family meeting](/toolbox/#family-meetings), such as no hurting and ask before you take."
- "Plan for the usual flashpoints: a timer for turns, a shelf for special things that don't have to be shared, separate seats in the car."
- "On calm days, practice the skills: naming feelings, asking for a turn, trading, walking away."
- "Avoid comparing your children, even to encourage them."
help:
- "One child is regularly hurt, frightened or picked on, or the aggression mostly goes one way."
- "Fights often cause injuries or involve objects used as weapons."
- "A child seems anxious, sad or withdrawn, or starts dreading time at home. In a national sample, sibling aggression in the past year was linked with more mental health distress in children and teens.[[cite:tucker-2013]]"
- "Nothing changes after several weeks of a consistent plan. See [when it is not working](/approach/#not-working)."

Siblings share space, toys and their parents' attention, so disagreements come with the territory. Hurting each other is a different matter, and it deserves to be taken seriously. In a national sample of 3,599 children and youth, those who had experienced sibling aggression in the past year (psychological, property or physical) reported more mental health distress, and among children up to age 9, even mild physical assault was linked with more distress than among older youth.<Cite id="tucker-2013" />

With coaching, children can learn to sort out conflicts themselves. When mothers of 5- to 8-year-olds were trained to mediate their children's disputes, the children reasoned, talked about feelings and understood each other's motives more often than children in control families, and younger siblings in particular were better able to solve conflicts.<Cite id="siddiqui-ross-2004" /> The study looked only at short-term effects. In another program, 4- to 8-year-old siblings who were taught emotional and social skills got along better and handled their emotions better than children on a wait list.<Cite id="kennedy-kramer-2008" /> For more, see <L to="/approach/#siblings">siblings</L> in the best-proven approach.
